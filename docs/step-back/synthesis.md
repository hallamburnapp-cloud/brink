# Synthesis

**diagnosis.** The redesign bolted Wordle's one-attempt-a-day onto Reigns' learning curve, so a stranger's only attempt is the tutorial: the calm bot reaches dawn 61% of nights but the random bot 7.6%, none of the four harness nights made it, and the first thing most new players see after losing a game nobody explained is a 19-hour countdown and a price. The theme caps the audience before a single mechanic is judged: a Le Carré register, three beige republics, a red NUCLEAR WAR stamp on every share card in docs/playtests/night and two-thirds of all 94 endings written as losses is a game for strategy-literate men (a market of tens of thousands, not the wide base you asked for), and nobody posts "☢️ 5:59" to their mum. The 451 cards were re-voiced but never re-set, so the deck contradicts the only number on screen (card one says "cut at 3:40" under a 3:00 clock; the crisis says "ten past eleven" at 5:27 with a "+2 political capital" toast), and a night is eight arcs touched and none resolved, ending in dice at a clock pinned on 5:59 that kill 38% of careful arrivals over who gets a chair. A swipe has no payoff: a fill moves three pixels, the outcome prose already written for every gamble is never displayed, and the only teaching mechanism (dots on the dials) fires on a drag that phone players who tap the two big buttons never make, so nothing connects cause to effect and every loss reads as the game cheating. The frame then fails every transition a phone player makes (Home is 11px grey text on the run screen and the last of six buttons on a result screen that cannot be reopened once left, the strip is fifty unlabeled squares, Record lists DEFCON and NC3, the purchase has no price on any screen), and your "can't get back to the home screen" is the symptom of a design that treated leaving as a forfeit.

**recommendation.** Build BRINK as the night desk of The Brink Hotel: a grand, slightly too big seaside hotel on a cliff (the name becomes the joke, so the brand, mark, URL, OG image and Stripe copy all survive), where you are the night manager, it is 3am, the phone is ringing, and you have until the Day Manager walks in at 6:00 to keep GUESTS, STAFF, MONEY and THE BUILDING off the floor, after which a guest writes your review out of five stars and that review is what you post. It is the synthesis of the three hotel concepts, which every judge placed at the top (Night Shift, NIGHT DESK and THE BRINK each won one scorecard at 47; no political concept scored above 44 with anyone), and I am choosing it because the hotel is the one setting that keeps everything the four critiques said to keep (the 3am phone, the clock as the only number, the two-sentence voice, the Partner with soup, the cast's voices transliterated one-for-one, the engine, the pipeline, the share renderer, the commerce plumbing) while removing the three things they called fatal: the theme ceiling, the one-attempt tutorial, and the dice climax. It is a transposition of your game, not a replacement of it: the Hotline becomes The Majestic across the square ("courtesies are remembered"), the General becomes the Chef ("I'm not saying the night manager forgot the eggs; I'm saying somebody did"), the false-alarm arc becomes THE ALARM (the panel says fire in Room 9, the toaster in 3B), and the Partner's soup line survives verbatim. Grafts, by source: from NIGHT DESK, the one-for-one cast transliteration so speakers.yaml voices and the agent prompts carry over, a practice night first so Tonight is never the tutorial, authored review quotes keyed on flags so the quote names tonight's idea without spoiling it, and the ten-stranger gate before any interface work; from Night Shift, a fixed card count so the clock reaches 6:00 by construction, no roll may carry an ending, pointerdown-preview and pointerup-commit with a one-time "hold to peek", "Tomorrow: THE DOG SHOW" on the review and Home, one covered night a week, and the brand kept as the hotel; from THE BRINK, four bars where only empty kills and full triggers a comedy card, mood faces on the speakers whose regard drives the odds word, one memory flag per regular carried into the next night, and the faint "↩ lights off, 3:40" stamp on consequence cards; from Twelve Calls, the purchase moment (the review re-renders live with your name as you type, "Keep it · £2.99") and AGAIN on the same seed; from Tonight, yesterday's night free with unlimited tries and today's review reopenable from Home all day; from Back to Bed, a reaction line on every choice and "what everyone did" on the three cards that mattered, from a cookie-free KV counter. The build is about 25 engineer-days with content agents from day two and a human comedy pass in week five; the fatal risk is the same one every hotel concept named, that the cards are pleasant rather than funny, so the gate is non-negotiable: eight cards plus twenty more and four mocked reviews go on ten strangers' phones on days three and four, and if fewer than seven ask for card nine the cards are rewritten before a line of interface is written.

**runner up.** BRINK — Tonight: nine fixed calls from 3:00 to 5:40, three tries, a seed-built night the solver has proved fair (a dawn path exists, the greedy all-green path fails, a perfect dawn exists), effect chips on the buttons so tap players are never blind, ① ② badges on the second try, the path revealed in nine lines after the third fall, and a 3×9 grid with a 1/2/3/X number that reads like Wordle's; grafted with Twelve Calls' reply flip on every choice and its name-in-the-headline purchase with a live preview. It is the better bet in exactly two cases. First, if you decide the geopolitical fiction is non-negotiable: it keeps the world, drops nuclear war and every proper noun from the daily, and is the only political concept whose appeal does not depend on jokes landing, because its hook is deduction and a directly comparable number. Second, if the hotel fails the stranger gate twice (cards still read as competent rather than quotable after one rewrite), since a puzzle survives flat prose where a sitcom cannot. Take it knowing its ceiling is news-readers (tens of thousands, not hundreds) and its own fatal risk: a 160-call pool constrained to yield a fair, non-greedy night every day will start rhyming within weeks, and a trap that is not foreshadowed in the text reads as the game lying.
