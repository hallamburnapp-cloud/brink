# Six concepts

## BRINK — Back to Bed

**one line.** It's 3am, you run the country, the phone is ringing, and everyone who calls wants to wake the world; you have eighteen calls to get everyone back to bed by six.

**audience.** Primary: the Wordle / Connections / NYT Games crowd, 30–55, both sexes, who read a little news and recognise the comedy of being the only grown-up awake at 3am (parents get this instantly). They want a two-minute daily they can finish, win more often than not, laugh at once, and paste to a group chat without it being grim. Secondary: the Reigns and strategy crowd the current LAUNCH.md already targets; they come for the engine and the thriller, and they are the ones who buy. Honest size: a text-heavy, English-language, politically framed daily does not reach Wordle's tens of millions. If one share loop lands it can hold 50–200k monthly actives; at a 1–2% buy rate on £2.99 (plus a later £2.99 app-store build) that is a modest income, not a company. Why THIS for a wide base: nobody else is making a comedy about tiredness and responsibility, the cast is already written and quotable, and 'get everyone back to bed by six' is a goal a stranger understands with no rules, while 'keep the dials off the edges' is not.

**theme and tone.** Same world, same three powers, same 3am phone, different register: Yes Minister at three in the morning, in a dressing gown. The player is the most powerful person in the country and the only one who wants everyone to calm down; every caller is wide awake and certain. Stakes are real (a frigate, a trawler, a missile track, a vote) but the texture is human and dry: soup, a hat, a note for 6:00 that says 'sleep'. Nuclear war still exists as the thing everyone is trying not to do, and the fuse that leads to it is the one object on screen that looks dangerous, but it ends well under one night in ten and is never a red stamp on a share card. The common bad ending is losing your job before breakfast, which is funny and shareable; the common good ending is a morning. The deck obeys the clock: every card happens between 3:00 and 6:00 tonight, no Mondays, no midnight deadlines, no summits in week two (a lint rule bans the vocabulary; agents re-set all 451 cards in a day). Named people appear by role only ('Your Partner', 'The Hotline'); real names and dossiers are a collectible, not a tax on card one.

**core loop.** Every 10 seconds: read one card from one of ~20 recurring characters; glance at the four gauges where small ghost ticks already show what each choice will do (no gesture needed); tap or swipe; the card flips to the speaker's one-line reaction while the needles swing visibly and the fuse hisses or shortens; the clock ticks forward ten minutes. Every run: eighteen cards, 3:00 to 6:00, always eighteen, the clock never freezes and never lies. Each night is ONE situation (The Cables, The Ghost on the Radar, The Man on the Ferry, The Black Sky, The Summit, The Boat That Wouldn't Turn: the existing arcs and flashpoints, ~15 of them) with a forced three-beat spine, entry in the 3 o'clock hour, it gets worse in the 4 o'clock hour (and the Partner always calls), the decision in the 5 o'clock hour, resolved by the ending. Six cards of the eighteen come from the shared pool (Press, Opposition, Partner, advisors) so two nights on the same situation differ. Reach 6:00 with no needle in the red and you get a morning; a needle in the red ends it with a named ending at that time; the fuse burning to the end is the war. Every day: open the app, see tonight's situation named on the board ('TONIGHT: THE CABLES'), play the one shared night, get a morning or a headline, see what percentage of everyone else did what you did on the cards that mattered, share the 3×6 grid and the quote, play Any Night free as many times as you like.

**first 60 seconds.** 0s, Home: navy screen, a big clock face reading 3:00 in serif, under it in mono 'TONIGHT · THE CABLES', one paper button 'ANSWER THE PHONE', and below it, smaller, 'Any night · practise, no record'. A thin streak line if you have one. Nothing else above the fold; a '···' top right holds Settings, About, Record. 3s, tap: the clock face slides up into the header and the screen becomes the run: header (← Home, 3:00, ···), the fuse under it (a thin dark bar labelled THE BRINK with a short ember at its left end), four gauges in a row (PEOPLE, ARMY, ALLIES, MONEY, each a horizontal track with red at both ends and a needle near the middle), the first card, two choice buttons. No overlay, no intro sheet, no timer. 5s, card one is Your Private Secretary: 'It is three in the morning, the phone is ringing, and I have put it on hold so you can find a dressing gown. Line one is the Chancellor; line two is your Partner, with soup.' Choices: 'Chancellor first' / 'Soup first'. On the PEOPLE and MONEY gauges two small ticks sit either side of the needles, one blue (left button's edge colour), one amber (right's). Without being told, the player sees that each answer moves two needles in opposite directions. 12s, tap 'Soup first': the card flips to a one-line reaction, 'You take the soup. The Chancellor can hear you drinking it, and says so.' The PEOPLE needle swings right with a soft tick, MONEY swings left, the clock turns to 3:10. 15s, card two is the Chancellor explaining the cables; the ticks on MONEY are bigger this time. 25s, card three the General offering a frigate; the ARMY ticks are large and the fuse shows a faint amber ghost extension for the frigate option: the player learns that force warms the fuse. 40s, card four the Press. 50s, the first odds card (the Spy Chief's trawler): the choice reads 'Name the boat on television · Even', the word Even tinted by the fuse's current colour; tap, a short roll overlay, HELD or FAILED, then the outcome prose that already exists in the YAML ('a skipper of seventy-one, now on television, waving'). 60s, the clock reads 3:50, five cards in, the player knows what the gauges are, what the fuse is, that gambles exist and why one was Even, and has laughed once. The home button has been visible the whole time; tapping it saves and leaves, no confirm, and Home shows 'Pick the phone back up · 3:50'.

**screen.** Top to bottom on the run screen. Header: '← Home' (44pt tap target, paper text), the clock in the centre as the only number on screen (3:40, serif, ticks up ten minutes per card and never caps), '···' on the right for pause/mute/rules. The fuse: a full-width thin bar labelled THE BRINK, dark with a glowing ember that grows from the left toward the right edge; its colour (green, amber, red) is also the colour of every odds word on the cards, so the rule 'calm night, kinder gambles' is visible without a sentence. Four gauges in a row (PEOPLE, ARMY, ALLIES, MONEY): horizontal tracks with red zones at both ends and a needle, the universal temperature-gauge grammar, so 'middle good, either end bad' needs no explaining; the DANGER tile is gone, its role taken by the fuse, which looks like a different object because it is one. At rest every gauge a choice would move shows two ghost ticks, blue for the left choice and amber for the right, sized by magnitude; hidden costs show as a hollow tick. The card: portrait, role as the headline ('Your Chancellor'), two sentences of 20px serif on paper, the time in the corner. Two choice buttons, left edged blue, right edged amber, 72px, with the odds word where there is one. What moves on a swipe or drag: the card tilts, that side's ticks grow and the other side's fade; on commit the card flips to the reaction line, the needles swing with a tick sound, any gauge whose needle entered red pulses, the fuse's ember lengthens or shortens with a hiss, the clock advances. On a tap the same happens with no drag. Effects in the simple ruleset are scaled ×2 (the existing cost-scale knob) so a typical swipe moves a needle 10–15% of its track and is unmistakable.

**feedback after a swipe.** Three things, in under 1.5 seconds, none of them a number. One: the card flips to the speaker's reaction, one line in their voice ('"Alone," he says, and the word goes down in a notebook with your name on the cover'); every choice in the deck gets one (~900 lines, agents, ≤90 characters, validator-enforced), and odds cards show the success/failure prose that is already written and currently never displayed (RollResult gains a text field; run.ts reads outcome.text). The reaction holds until the player taps or 1.4s. Two: the needles swing, visibly, 10–15% of the track for a normal card, with a tick per gauge; a needle crossing into red makes that gauge's frame pulse and the clock tick louder for the next card. Three: the fuse's ember grows or shrinks with a hiss, and if it grows past a band the odds words on the next card change colour. Over a night the player connects cause to effect every ten seconds, which is the whole of learning the game, and the ending screen then quotes back the card where it went wrong ('The moment: 4:40, you sent the frigate').

**why people share.** The shareable object is the morning card: one line and a grid a stranger can read in a chat. Text: 'BRINK #41 · THE CABLES / 🌅 6:00 · "Sleep" / 🟩🟩🟩🟨🟩🟩 / 🟩🟨🟧🟩🟩🟩 / 🟩🟩🟩🟨🟩🟩 / brink.app'. Three rows are the three hours (3am, 4am, 5am), six cells per row are the six cards of that hour, so eighteen cells at most; a cell's colour is how close any needle was to red after that card (green comfortable, yellow one in the warning band, orange one in the red band), the cell you fell on is 🟥 and the rest of the night is ⬜. The shape tells the story Wordle's does: a 4:50 loss is two rows and a red cell, a hairy win is a yellow-orange middle row that comes back green. The headline is the ending name in quotes, which differs between players on the same night ('I got The Soup, what did you get?'), and because every player read the same eighteen cards the reply is a conversation ('you sent the FRIGATE?'). The PNG keeps the paper, serif and big clock from the current share card, drops the red NUCLEAR WAR stamp (the stamp reads MORNING or the time you fell), carries the full moment quote untruncated, and the 3×6 grid. On the morning screen itself, under the result for the daily: 'On the trawler: 71% named the boat. You waited.' for the three cards that mattered most (a Cloudflare Worker KV counter per daily/card/side, no accounts, cookie-free), which is both the reason to share and the reason to come back tomorrow.

**why people pay.** One purchase, 'The Red Phone', £2.99 once (the price printed on every screen that mentions it), through the existing Stripe link, Worker and restore-by-email. It unlocks Rewind: on any Any Night ending, a button that reads 'Rewind to 4:40', the card the engine knows the night turned on, so a loss becomes 'I know what I'd do differently' instead of a shuffle (run state is already serialisable per card). It also unlocks the Archive (replay any past daily you missed, and your own), the Dossier (every morning you have collected with the full text, plus the cast's names and files), Send a Night (a seed link: 'play my exact night'), and, as a footnote, the long game (Expert) for those who ask. The moment it is offered: never on a player's first two nights, never on the daily's loss screen; on the third Any Night loss the ending screen shows 'Try again' (free, primary) beside 'Rewind to 4:40 · £2.99 once' (secondary), and after a player's first morning a quiet card: 'You have a story. Keep it.' Free stays free forever: the daily and unlimited Any Night, so nobody is buying the tutorial and nobody is told to come back tomorrow after a loss they did not understand.

**why people return.** Tonight has a name, so it is news: 'TONIGHT: THE GHOST ON THE RADAR' is a reason to open the app that 'Play tonight' is not, and ~15 situations × 3 seats × a varying shared pool means weeks before a night rhymes. The cast: the Partner calls every night in the 4 o'clock hour and has a different thing to say about soup, the Hotline remembers courtesies, the General does not say it, the Private Secretary's 6:00 note changes; people come back for characters, not systems. The streak counts mornings reached, which is a streak worth protecting because careful players reach morning more often than not (target: calm bot 55–70%, random bot under 15%). The comparison: 'what did everyone else do on the frigate card' is only visible after you play. The collection: mornings you have had, shown as cards you own, never as 82 grey rows you have not. And an interruption costs nothing: Home saves the night, Home shows 'Pick the phone back up · 4:20', the daily is never forfeited by putting the phone away, and today's result stays reopenable and shareable from Home until midnight.

**what we reuse.** As-is: the deterministic engine (seeds, meters, hidden values, conditions/flags/follow-ups, odds, warning true/false branches, forced endings, per-card serialisable state, 316 tests); the YAML pipeline, zod schema and validator with the voice contract as lint; all 451 cards and 94 endings as raw material (re-set and re-tagged, not thrown away); the ~15 arcs and flashpoints as the night's situations; the speakers.yaml cast and portraits; the daily UTC seed, seat rotation, streak storage and saved-run resume in store.ts; the share PNG renderer's paper, serif, clock and typography; the roll overlay's HELD/FAILED language and the danger-to-odds link; Stripe Payment Link → Worker → signed offline token, restore by email, PWA build, itch zip, Capacitor/Tauri scaffolds, cookie-free analytics; the simulator with bot policies and the targets-before-tuning discipline (new M1–M5 targets); the Playwright harness that plays from the DOM, with the dove/hawk/balanced/gambler styles; the synthesised audio; the palette, type and scanlines.

**what we build or rewrite.** 1. Engine, simple ruleset v3 (3 days): fixed eighteen-card night at ten minutes per card, clock never caps; night deck builder takes one situation (arc or flashpoint) plus six shared-pool cards and forces the spine (entry slot in cards 1–3, development in 7–12, resolution in 15–18, Partner card in the 4 o'clock hour); effect scale ×2; at most two odds cards and one timed card (15s, no heartbeat until 5s) per night; fuse replaces danger in the UI model; Rewind as 'restore state at card N'. 2. Reaction lines (1 day engineering, 1 day agents): schema gains react per choice (≤90 chars) and RollResult gains text; run.ts surfaces outcome.text; validator requires both; agents write ~900 reactions in each speaker's voice. 3. Re-set and simplify all 451 cards (1 day agents, 0.5 day lint): everything happens tonight between 3:00 and 6:00; banned words extended with Monday–Sunday, midnight, noon, tomorrow, 'day twelve', week; at most one fictional place name per card; choice labels must name the action and its object; each card tagged to a situation. 4. Endings (0.5 day agents): kinds relabelled to match text (a dawn that reads as a failure is not a dawn), new kind morning, stamps MORNING or FELL AT, the moment quote stored full length. 5. Run screen (4 days): header with Home/clock/menu, the fuse, four needle gauges with both-side ghost ticks and hollow hidden-cost ticks, the reaction flip, scaled needle swings with audio, no overlays over the speaker's name, pause via menu, timer only on the one card. 6. Home as the board (1 day): clock face, tonight's situation, Answer the phone, Any Night, resume line, today's result with Share, streak, '···'. 7. Morning screen (2 days + 1 day Worker KV): header with Home and Share at the top, ending name, two paragraphs, the full moment quote, the 3×6 grid, 'what everyone did' for three cards, persisted so it reopens from Home all day. 8. Share (1 day): 3×6 text grid and PNG on the existing template, no war stamp, full quote, real domain. 9. Monetisation (2 days): price strings everywhere, Rewind button and offer placement rules, Stripe opened in the same tab with return handling, restore copy fixed, 16px inputs and pinch-zoom restored. 10. Strip Expert from free screens (1 day): Record shows nights, mornings, streak, favourite caller; compendium becomes Mornings (owned only); Expert lives in Settings as 'The long game'. 11. Balance (2 days): M1 calm bot morning 55–70%, M2 random under 15%, M3 war under 8% of calm nights, M4 median night eighteen cards ≈ two minutes at 6s/card, M5 at least six distinct endings per situation across 5,000 nights; harness updated for the new DOM and asserting a one-sentence retellable transcript per style. 12. Onboarding by the first card (0.5 day): the Private Secretary's opener teaches by doing, no intro sheet. Total about 19 engineer-days plus three agent-days of content; one strong engineer ships it in four weeks.

**sample cards.**

- 3:00 · YOUR PRIVATE SECRETARY — It is three in the morning, the phone is ringing, and I have put it on hold so you can find a dressing gown. Line one is the Chancellor; line two is your Partner, with soup. ← Chancellor first ↩ He says 'at last' in a voice that means 'finally'. The soup goes cold on principle. → Soup first ↩ You take the soup. The Chancellor can hear you drinking it, and says so.
- 3:10 · YOUR CHANCELLOR — Somebody cut three cables under the Straits tonight, and a fifth of our internet now goes the long way round. The markets open at nine, and I would like to be asleep when they do. ← Markets shut. Call it maintenance ↩ 'Maintenance,' he repeats, writing it down like a name he will have to spell to a judge. → Open at nine. Say nothing ↩ He says 'nine' once, in the voice of a man booking his own funeral.
- 3:30 · YOUR GENERAL — The cable ship is a navy ship if I say so, and I can have her over the breaks by five with a frigate beside her. I do not say we should; I am saying I can. ← Send the frigate too ↩ 'Thank you,' he says, which from him is a parade. → Send the cable ship alone ↩ 'Alone,' he says, and the word goes down in a notebook with your name on the cover.
- 3:50 · THE PRESS — I have forty seconds of a trawler with her lights off over the third cable, and a six o'clock bulletin to fill. 'Accident' or 'attack': pick the word, or I pick it. ← Say 'accident' ↩ 'Accident. Lovely. I'll put it next to the photo of the anchor.' → Say 'we are looking into it' ↩ 'Six words and no verb you'll regret.' She sounds almost disappointed in you.
- 4:10 · YOUR SPY CHIEF — The trawler's owner is a shell company whose only other asset is a warehouse in their capital. An accident, or exactly how you would arrange one. ← Name the boat on television · Even ↩ HELD: Their embassy denies it so fast the denial was clearly written yesterday. FAILED: She is a fishing boat with a bad tracker and a skipper of seventy-one, now on television, waving. → Wait for the divers ↩ The divers go down at first light. Until then the only fact is wet.
- 4:30 · YOUR PARTNER — There is soup, and a bed, which I mention because you seem to have forgotten where it is. Also the cat has been sick on the General's hat, which I have decided was a choice. ← Twenty minutes. Soup ↩ You eat standing up. It is the best twenty minutes of the night and the only ones without a phone in them. → Later. Save the hat ↩ 'Later,' he says. 'I'll tell the soup.'
- 5:00 · THE HOTLINE — 'The Premier regrets the hour and the cables, in that order, and asks whether a frigate at the breaks is a question or an answer.' A pause, then: 'He would take a question.' ← A question. Pull her back ↩ 'A question,' the voice repeats. 'Courtesies are remembered.' The line goes quiet in the good way. → An answer. Hold her there · Risky ↩ HELD: A long silence, then the formal closing; nothing happens, which at five in the morning is everything. FAILED: 'Then the Premier will give you one.' Two of their ships leave port before you have put the phone down.
- 5:50 · YOUR PRIVATE SECRETARY — The cable ship is over the breaks, the divers are down, and the Chancellor has stopped saying 'markets'. Your 6:00 is the Opposition leader, who would like to know what you did all night. ← Tell him everything ↩ He listens for eleven minutes and says he would have done the same, which he will deny by lunch. → Tell him you slept ↩ He believes you. It is the worst thing he has ever said about you.

**fatal risk.** That the night does not become a story. Everything above (the comparison, the quote, the share, the return) depends on eighteen cards reading as one situation with a beginning, a worse middle and a payoff; the current arcs were written for a three-week game with conditions and follow-ups spaced days apart, and if bending them into a forced spine starves the pool or leaves dead ends, the builder falls back to shuffle and we are back to 'it was going fine, then I got nuked over a chair' with better feedback. The test before any human sees it: four harness styles on the same seed must each produce a transcript a reader can retell in one sentence, and the ending must name a card the player actually saw. If three situations cannot pass that test after the agent pass, ship with the three that do and add the rest monthly rather than ship a shuffle.


## BRINK — Night Shift at the Brink Hotel

**one line.** You're the night manager of a grand old seaside hotel, it's 3am, the phone is ringing, everyone in the world is having the same terrible night as you, and in the morning your guests write your review.

**audience.** The NYT-games crowd, honestly: people who do Wordle or Connections on the bus, text the result to a group chat, and watch sitcoms at night. Skews 25–55, slightly female, English-first but the setting (a hotel, a wedding, a dog in the lift) needs no translation of culture, only of words. The content is safe to send to your mum: nobody dies, the worst outcome is a one-star review and your P45. Sizing without flattery: a web daily with a shareable review card can reach 50k–300k monthly players in year one if the review spreads through chats and screenshot accounts; it reaches millions only with press or a store feature. The hook that makes a stranger try it is a screenshot of a review ('Night manager Sam set fire to the lobby but was very polite about it ★★☆☆☆'), which is a thing people already post for fun. The current game's ceiling (strategy-literate men who read about geopolitics, tens of thousands) is removed by the theme, not by polish.

**theme and tone.** The Brink is a once-grand hotel on a cold seafront, 140 rooms, one lift that has opinions, a ballroom, a cellar, a fish tank in the lobby. You are its night manager, every night, the same person (no rotating seat). Every night a different Booking is in the building and it goes wrong: THE WEDDING PARTY, THE BAND, THE INSPECTOR, THE POWER CUT, THE SNOW, THE DOG SHOW, THE CONFERENCE OF MAGICIANS, THE STAG DO, THE MINOR ROYAL, THE FILM CREW, THE GHOST TOUR, THE SCHOOL TRIP (24 Bookings at launch). Tone: warm farce in the lineage of Fawlty Towers, The Grand Budapest Hotel and Only Murders in the Building: dry, kind, nobody is a villain, the comedy is people being reasonable in an unreasonable building. The existing voice contract survives intact (two sentences, 180 characters, concrete nouns, no jargon) and the existing best lines are the model ('a bed, which I mention because you seem to have forgotten where it is' is a hotel line already). Speakers by role, twelve of them: Your Porter, Your Chef, Your Cleaner, The Owner (always on the phone, always abroad), The Bride, The Guest in 212, The Couple in 310, The Drummer, The Health Inspector, The Dog (a Labrador; his cards are reported by the Porter), Your Partner (texts), and the night's Booking lead. Cards are set between 3:00 and 6:00 and may only refer to things that happen between 3:00 and 6:00 (breakfast at six, the day manager at six, the photographer at six); the validator bans 'tomorrow', 'Monday', 'noon', 'midnight', 'last week' from card text. Keep BRINK as the name: the hotel is The Brink, and the title card reads BRINK with 'Night shift' under it.

**core loop.** Every 7 seconds: someone is at the desk (portrait, role, two sentences), you hold a choice and the four faces tell you who will mind, you let go, the world answers in one sentence and the faces change, the clock moves on seven minutes. Every run: exactly 24 cards from 3:00 to 6:00, the first card always introduces tonight's Booking, roughly every third card is the Booking escalating in beats (the hose, the salmon, the ring in the fish tank), the rest are the regulars (the dog, 212, the Owner ringing, your Partner texting); the last hour (5:00–6:00, cards 17–24) is where the Booking comes to a head and the few rolls live, with odds as words made kinder by a low Chaos bar. The night ends on a dial (a face hits zero, or Chaos fills) or at 6:00 when the day manager walks in, and either way a guest writes your review: stars, one line, two paragraphs, the moment. Every day: open the app, see tonight's Booking name, play the one recorded night (three minutes), read the review, post it, see the streak and tomorrow's Booking teased ('Tomorrow: THE DOG SHOW'), and if you lost or want more, play Practice nights (random Bookings, unlimited, free, no record) until the loop is in your hands. Expert mode is gone from the product.

**first 60 seconds.** 0–4s, Home: navy, the BRINK mark, one serif line 'It's 3am. The phone is ringing.' and one huge paper button ANSWER IT. Under it, small: 'TONIGHT · #12 · THE WEDDING PARTY · SAME NIGHT FOR EVERYONE'. A second, quieter button: 'Practice night'. A footer row: Guest Book · Settings. Nothing else, no price, no seat, no countdown. 4–6s: tap ANSWER IT. A brief 'brrring' and the run screen rises: clock 3:00 top centre, a 44px HOME button top left, a thin grey bar labelled CHAOS under the clock (empty), four tiles with faces (GUESTS, STAFF, THE OWNER, THE TILL), each at a neutral expression and about two-thirds full. One line fades in over the card for three seconds and never returns: 'Keep them happy. Keep the chaos down. Make it to six.' 6–15s, card one, always the Booking's opener: Your Porter, 'The wedding in the ballroom has run out of champagne and the bride has found the fire hose...', two buttons: 'Wake the chef' / 'Let her have the hose'. A tiny 'hold to peek' hint pulses once under the buttons on the first card only. The player presses 'Wake the chef' and, while their thumb is down, the STAFF face scowls with ▼▼ over it and GUESTS smiles with ▲; they let go, the card slides off, a strip reads 'The chef says a word you will remember, and the champagne comes up at 3:12.', the Staff face settles into a frown, a soft lift-ding sounds, the clock reads 3:07. They have now seen cause and effect once without reading a rule. 15–40s, cards two to five: The Guest in 212 and the man in her bath; the Owner ringing from Marbella (the OWNER face is now obviously a person who can fire you); the Labrador who has learned the lift (pure joke card, both answers cheap, teaches that not every card is a trap); the Chef's forty vegetarians. By 40s the Chaos bar has moved once, in amber, with a tiny alarm chirp, and the player has learned without being told that it is the thing that must not fill. 40–60s, cards six to eight: a Partner text with soup, the second Booking beat (the bride has the cake upstairs), the Couple in 310 and the gong. Clock reads 3:52. No timer has appeared, no number but the clock, no name they had to learn, no proper noun that is not a room number. If their bus arrives they press HOME; the night is saved exactly where it is and Home says 'Pick the phone back up · 3:52'.

**screen.** Top to bottom on a phone. (1) Header, 48px: HOME at left as a real button (44px hit area, 12px mono caps, with a left arrow), the clock centred in 22px mono ('3:07'), nothing on the right. (2) The CHAOS bar: a full-width 10px horizontal bar directly under the header with a small bell glyph at its right end; it fills left to right, grey to amber to red, and is the only horizontal thing on the screen so it cannot be confused with the four tiles; full means the alarm goes and the night ends. (3) Four tiles in a row, each 72px: a face (GUESTS a couple, STAFF the porter's cap, THE OWNER a man on a phone, THE TILL a cash drawer with a face on the coin) whose expression has five states from delighted to furious, plus a thin fill ring around the tile; both the face and the ring say the same thing, and the only rule is they must not hit empty. Labels in 11px caps. (4) The card: paper on navy, 20px serif, portrait at left, role as the headline ('Your Chef'), no personal name anywhere, two sentences, a footer with the time. (5) Two choice buttons, 72px tall, labels in 18px serif, with an odds word in small caps at the right end when there is a roll ('risky'). (6) A 32px reply strip between card and buttons, empty until a choice lands. What moves on a swipe or a held button: the card tilts toward the chosen side with its label stamped on it, the tiles that will change show big ▲/▼ arrows (one to three by magnitude) and their face previews the new expression, the Chaos bar shows a ghost segment of how much it will grow, and the odds word highlights the Chaos bar to show the link. On release: the card flies off, the reply strip types one sentence, the faces animate to their new state with a bounce and a sound, the clock ticks forward seven minutes, and the next card rises after 1.2 seconds (tap to skip). In the last hour the clock turns amber and the header reads THE LAST HOUR; rolls show the roll overlay with HELD / FAILED, the outcome sentence, and the percentage only here. Nothing else: no menu, no toasts, no numbers, no seat label.

**feedback after a swipe.** Three channels inside 300ms, and one is always words. (a) The reply line: every choice carries a `then` sentence from the world, written as the punchline, shown in the strip under the card before the next card arrives. Hold 'Wake the chef' → release → 'The chef says a word you will remember, and the champagne comes up at 3:12.' Hold 'Let her have the hose' → release → 'The ballroom is now a water feature, and the bride has never been happier.' This is the outcome prose the current game writes and never shows, made mandatory on every choice, not only on rolls. (b) The faces: the tiles that moved change expression with a bounce and keep the new expression; a move of 10 or more also flashes the ring. Because the face is the value, a -12 is a visibly different person, not a 3px fill change. (c) Sound: a lift ding for a face going up, a dull clunk for down, an alarm chirp when Chaos rises, the bell when a roll is held. Before the swipe: pointerdown on either button or any drag of the card shows the preview (arrows, face previews, Chaos ghost), so tap players see the stake during their own press and learn to hold; the first card shows 'hold to peek' once. Rolls never end the night: a FAILED roll costs faces or Chaos and says so in its sentence, so every loss is a dial the player watched empty, and the review names it ('Fired at 4:37' is always the Owner's face at zero).

## BRINK — Twelve Calls

**one line.** It's 3am, the phone is ringing, and everyone in the world gets the same twelve calls tonight: take them, and see what tomorrow's front page says about you.

**audience.** People who already play one daily (Wordle, Connections, the Mini) and read the news with a raised eyebrow: 25–55, mixed gender, phone in hand on a bus or in bed. Secondary: everyone who played Reigns once. The register that reaches them is tired workplace comedy with real stakes (The Thick of It at 3am), not thriller; the nuclear endings move to Expert so a strip is safe to post in a family group. Honest size: this is a tens-of-thousands-of-daily-players game by default, low hundreds of thousands if the strips spread on their own, and only a viral strip gets it past that. The owner should expect a small business from the £2.99 unlock, not a Wordle.

**theme and tone.** You are the person in the chair; the country is never named and neither are you. Every call is a real person with a problem and two ways to answer it: the Partner with soup, the General who 'does not say it, is saying they say it', the Press with a photo, the Hotline with its pauses. Dry, concrete, human, never glib; the joke is always in what people say back to you, and the stakes are always your job, your ally, your money, your army and the heat in the room. The night is twelve calls from 3:00 to 6:00; every card happens inside those three hours, by rule and by validator. The worst thing that can happen in the free game is that the shooting starts at sea or you are out of the chair by breakfast; the mushroom cloud belongs to Expert.

**core loop.** Every 10 seconds: read a two-sentence call, tap a choice to lean (the card tilts, ghost needles show where the four gauges and the heat bar would move), tap it again or swipe to say it; the stamp slams, the card flips to the speaker's one-line reply, the needles slide with a visible trail, the clock flips forward fifteen minutes, the next call arrives. Every run (about two minutes): twelve calls, or fewer if a needle hits the red, then the front page: a headline ending, two short paragraphs, the four-panel strip of your night, Share, Again. Again replays the exact same night (same seed) so the second run is 'I know what I'd do differently', which is the Reigns loop and the learning curve the daily lacked. Every day: a new episode at midnight UTC (The Trawler, The Moon, The Birthday, The Summit...), the same twelve calls for everyone, your first run goes on the record, your friends' strips have the same opening panel and a different punchline.

**first 60 seconds.** 0s, Home is the board: navy screen, a big clock reading 3:00 AM, under it '#212 · THE TRAWLER · SAME NIGHT FOR EVERYONE', a pulsing phone glyph, one button: ANSWER. Nothing else above the fold (settings, record and the unlock are small text at the bottom). 2s: the card slides up. Header: a 44px '← Home' top-left, the clock centre. Below: four short horizontal gauges with a needle each, centred, both ends shaded red, labelled with a face/shield/handshake/coin glyph and the words PEOPLE, ARMY, ALLIES, MONEY; under them a thin thermometer bar labelled HEAT, blue, a fifth full. The card: Duty Officer portrait, 'The Duty Officer', two sentences about a fishing boat and three cut cables. Two buttons: 'Board the boat' / 'Follow her. Say nothing'. 8s: the player taps 'Board the boat'. The card tilts left, the ARMY needle shows a ghost to the right, the ALLIES needle a ghost to the left, HEAT a ghost upward, and the button now reads 'Board the boat · tap again'. 11s: second tap. Red stamp BOARDED slams on the card, the card flips, the Duty Officer replies in one line, the needles slide to their ghosts with a trail, HEAT grows with a pulse, the clock flips to 3:15. 15–60s: the Chancellor (markets), the Partner (soup), the Press (the photo). By the end of the first minute the player has watched four replies land and four needle moves they caused, has never seen a number, an overlay, a timer or a tutorial, and knows the rule without being told it: keep the needles out of the red and the heat off the top. If a needle hits red at 3:45 the card flips to a headline and the front page appears with AGAIN as the first button; the whole thing cost ninety seconds.

**screen.** Top to bottom. Header: '← Home' (44px hit area, always present, leaving keeps the night exactly where it is, no confirm, no forfeit); the clock, the only number on screen, large; '#212' small right. Gauges: four horizontal tracks in a row, each with a glyph and word above, a needle on the track, red shading at both ends: the grammar is 'keep it in the middle', readable without colour. Heat: one full-width thin bar under them, labelled HEAT, filling left to right, blue to red, bad at one end only, because thermometers are. Card: paper on navy, portrait, role as the headline (no personal names anywhere in the night), two sentences in 20px serif, the time in the corner. Choices: two 72px buttons, left and right. On a lean (tap once or drag): the card tilts, the choice text appears as a stamp, the needles show hollow ghost markers where they would land, the heat bar shows a hatched extension. On commit (second tap, or swipe past the threshold): the stamp slams, the card flips to its reply face (same portrait, one line in 22px serif, the stamp small in the corner), needles slide to their ghosts leaving a fading trail, heat grows with a pulse and a tick, the clock flips. Nothing else: no menu dot, no timer bar in the free night, no toasts, no '+2 anything'.

**feedback after a swipe.** Three things, in order, inside 900ms. First the stamp: your choice in red mono across the card ('BOARDED', 'NO COMMENT', 'TWO HOURS'), which is also the panel caption in the strip. Second the reply: the card flips and the speaker answers you in one line, written per choice, which is where every joke and every warning lives ('Boarded. Her hold is full of fish and one very new radio.'). This is the content the current game has written for its odds cards and never shows; here every card has two. Third the needles: they slide from where they were to where they are, with the ghost already shown during the lean, so cause and effect are the same picture a second apart; a needle entering the red shading pulses once and the gauge's word turns red; the heat bar grows visibly and the room tint warms a step. A roll (the few odds cards that remain) shows HELD or FAILED on the flipped face before the reply, with the odds word on the choice beforehand.

## BRINK — Tonight (the daily is "Tonight"; the brand stays BRINK)

**one line.** It's 3am, the phone rings nine times before dawn, and everyone in the world gets the same nine calls: tap your way to 6:00 in as few tries as you can, then post your three-row grid.

**audience.** The NYT-games crowd, not the grand-strategy crowd: people who do Wordle or Connections on the bus and send the grid to a group chat. Skews 25–55, half women, zero knowledge of geopolitics required or rewarded. The hook they already understand is Reigns' "you are the ruler, make the call" and the famous "3am phone call" (which is why Reigns reached millions while Le Carré reaches a few per cent); what capped BRINK was register and mushroom clouds, not the premise. Honest sizing: a well-executed web daily with a legible grid can reach 20–80k monthly players organically and a few hundred thousand only with a viral break; plan the business on 30–50k MAU, not millions.

**theme and tone.** Same frame, same cast, rewritten register. You are the one who has to answer the phone tonight; the country is never named and the map never appears (it is "the neighbours", "the island", "our ally", "the border"). The same advisors call every night, by role, so they become running characters the way Reigns' dog and devil do: Your General ("I don't say it, I'm saying they say it"), Your Chancellor (numbers, then a dry joke), the Duty Officer, the Press, the Hotline (translated, with pauses), the Opposition (reasonable, lethal), and Your Partner, who asks if you have eaten. Tone is a tired, funny, human 3am, never a memo: every card is a person with a problem and the cards never mention a day, a week or a time outside 3:00–6:00. Losses are human and quotable (impeached, the General stops answering, the allies stop calling, the banks don't open, the first shot), and nuclear war is removed from the daily entirely; the ☢️ stamp lives in Expert only. A loss is 🌑 and a time; a win is 🌅. Nothing on the share would embarrass anyone's mum.

**core loop.** Every 10 seconds: read one call (two sentences, no proper nouns), look at the two answers and the arrows under them (each answer shows exactly which gauges it moves, arrows coloured red if that move takes a gauge toward its red end right now, green if toward the middle), tap one, watch the beads slide and the speaker react in one line, clock ticks twenty minutes. Every run (a "try"): nine calls, 3:00 to 5:40, dawn at 6:00, about 70 seconds; a gauge touching red ends the try instantly with a named ending and an "Again" button that restarts tonight at 3:00 with your earlier answers marked ① ② on the buttons, so the second try is deduction, not memory. Three tries per night, Wordle's six guesses. The puzzle is that the night is a fixed, seed-built tree: the same nine calls play out the same way for everyone, some answers echo two or three calls later (marked ⏳), one call's hidden truth (the missile is the moon, the defector is a plant) is fixed for tonight, and the greedy "all green arrows" path is built to fail. A solver proves every published night has at least one dawn path, that the greedy path falls, and that a "perfect dawn" (all five beads in the centre band at 6:00) exists. Every day: Home is today's board; you play until dawn or three falls, you get your grid, you share it, your 1/2/3/X distribution grows, and tomorrow's nine calls arrive at midnight UTC.

**fatal risk.** That the tries feel like trial-and-error instead of deduction. Wordle gives exact information after every guess; here the information about a call's echo arrives only by playing into it, so if the night-builder's "trap" is not foreshadowed in the text (the ⏳ chip, the Press saying "it runs at six", the Duty Officer's two minutes), the second and third tries become memorising a maze and the grid loses its brag. The guard is a content rule the validator cannot fully check: every echo must be announced in words on the card that sets it, and the solver must reject any seed whose only dawn paths depend on an un-announced echo. If a week of harness and human playtests shows first-try dawns under 10% or over 40%, or players describing it as "guessing", the puzzle is wrong and no amount of polish on the gauges saves it.


## THE BRINK (The Brink Hotel, night desk) — brand, mark and URL kept; the title becomes the joke: a seaside hotel on a cliff edge, built 1931, please don't lean on the railings.

**one line.** You're the night manager of a crumbling seaside hotel: keep eight impossible people happy till breakfast, then read the review they leave you.

**audience.** Adults 25–55, skewing female, who play Wordle/Connections/Royal Match and watch sitcoms; the people who post a funny screenshot to a family group chat. Second ring: Reigns and narrative-game fans (who find it through the mechanics) and UK/Commonwealth players who recognise the seaside-hotel register, though nothing in it needs local knowledge. Honestly sized: a web-first daily with a share loop reaches tens of thousands of monthly players in its first months if the review card gets passed around, low hundreds of thousands if it catches on one platform (TikTok screen recordings of a bad night are the likely vector); millions only with app-store placement later. Paying: 1.5–3% of people who have played five nights, at £2.99. That is a modest living, not a lottery ticket, and it is at least ten times the ceiling of the geopolitics version, whose every channel in LAUNCH.md was a nerd channel.

**theme and tone.** The setting moves, from a 3am geopolitical crisis to the night shift at a failing British seaside hotel. Why: three independent critiques and the owner reached the same verdict from different directions. The theme caps the audience (nobody posts ☢️ NUCLEAR WAR to their mum, and two-thirds of all endings are losses stamped in red), the register is Le Carré (a 5% audience even when the sentences are short), the deck cannot obey its own clock because it was written for three weeks, and the owner's goal is a wide base. The engine does not care what the cards are about. What survives of the old game is its shape, which was always right: it is the middle of the night, the phone is ringing, a clock is the only number, a cast of people with agendas want opposite things from you, and someone at home asks if you have eaten. A hotel at night is the natural home for every one of those: the night is literally one night (11:00 PM to 7:00 AM, so the clock is finally true), the phone really does ring at 3am, the cast is already in the building, and breakfast is a finish line everyone understands. Tone: warm comedy, Fawlty Towers by way of Schitt's Creek, affectionate about everyone including the villains. The worst thing that can happen is a flood, a walkout and a one-star review. The old voice contract carries over whole (two sentences, concrete nouns, wit not jokes, the Partner's soup) with stricter limits: 140 characters, first names only, room numbers instead of place names, and a validator lint that bans any time word outside tonight (Monday, tomorrow, noon, next week) and any clock time outside the card's window. Visual identity kept: the paper card, serif/mono pairing, portraits, red stamp, mono caps; navy becomes a deep midnight green with brass accents, a one-token change.

**core loop.** Every 10 seconds: a face and one line (≤140 characters) arrive; you tap a choice to see who it pleases, who it annoys and which of four bars moves, then swipe or tap again to commit; the bars jump (never less than 8 points on a 100-point bar, so you see it), the speaker's face changes mood, and they answer you in one line before the next person arrives. Every run: 24 cards exactly, 11:00 PM to 7:00 AM at 20 minutes each, two to three minutes of real time. One Situation per night (the hen party, the power cut, the inspector, the funeral party, the storm, the dog show) escalates in three or four beats spaced through the night with the cast taking sides, and resolves at breakfast into a review written by one of them. Dying is a review too, and the restart is one tap. Every day: Tonight's Booking, the same night for everyone, one recorded attempt, a review to compare with friends (stars and which faces you lost), a streak of nights worked, and regulars who remember what you did last night and bring it up.

**fatal risk.** The cards are not actually funny. ## NIGHT DESK (the hotel is The Grand)

**one line.** You're the night manager of a grand old hotel, it's 3am, the phone is ringing, and you have until breakfast to keep the guests happy, the staff sane, the money in the till, the name out of the papers and your own nerve intact; at 6:00 a guest reviews your night out of five stars, and that review is what you post.

**audience.** The Wordle / Connections / Heardle crowd and the people who bounced off Reigns after two kings: phone players 25–55, both sexes, who want two funny minutes on a bus and a result they can drop in the family group chat without anyone wincing. A hotel at 3am is a sitcom everyone already understands (Fawlty Towers, Grand Budapest, Hotel Babylon); nobody needs to know what the Sorrel Straits are, and "there is a swan in the bath" is safe to send to your mum, which "☢️ NUCLEAR WAR 5:59" was never going to be. Honestly sized: the current theme tops out at tens of thousands of strategy-literate men; this can plausibly reach 50k–300k monthly players in year one if the review card gets posted, and millions only with a viral break we cannot plan for. Money, honestly: at 100k monthly players, roughly 30% reach their third night, and 3% of those buy a £2.99 key (net ~£2.50) ≈ £2,000–2,500 a month plus whatever an app-store build adds later. That is a real small business, not a lottery ticket, and it is an order of magnitude more than the current funnel can produce. The engine already exists, so the cost side is content and interface days, not years.

**theme and tone.** One hotel, The Grand, old, slightly too big for its staff, with The Majestic across the square as the rival that is always "fully booked". One protagonist: you, the night manager, never named, never shown. The cast transliterates one-for-one from the existing speakers so the voice contract and the agent prompts carry over: the General becomes the Chef (clipped, certain, hides cost in the passive voice, "I do not say it, I am saying they say it"); the Spy Chief becomes the Concierge (hedged to the exact degree of confidence, knows everything, never says "I think"); the Hotline becomes Head Office (formal, delayed by a beat, the pauses are the message); the Press becomes the Critic in 212; the Opposition becomes the Deputy Manager (reasonable in tone, lethal in timing, "I've brought my own pen"); the Duty Officer becomes the Night Porter (reads the board, times and room numbers); the Scientist becomes Maintenance (explains the boiler as if it were weather); the Ambassador becomes the Doorman (every anecdote is a warning); the Ally's Prime Minister becomes the Bride's Mother; the Peace Movement becomes the yoga retreat in the ballroom; the Attorney General becomes the Insurance Man; the Chancellor becomes the Owner (numbers, consequences, then a dry joke); the rival leader becomes the Night Manager of The Majestic; and the Partner stays exactly the Partner, soup and all. Tone: dry, warm, quick, English-sitcom rather than American-wacky, wit not jokes, two sentences, concrete nouns (eggs, towels, room 412, a swan). Nobody dies. The worst thing that can happen is the hotel is in the paper, the staff walk out, the Owner closes the doors, or you leave your keys on the desk at 4:35 and walk into the car park, and every one of those is written as a punchline. The 3am frame, the clock as the only number, the paper-on-navy identity, the serif/mono pairing, the portraits, the red stamp and the ending format (emoji, headline, two paragraphs, the moment) all stay. The nuclear shadow, the three beige republics and every proper noun that is not a room number go. Expert mode leaves the product entirely: the code stays dormant, nothing on any screen refers to it.

**core loop.** Every 10 seconds: someone in the hotel has a problem, told in two sentences; you tilt the card or tap a side; dots light on the bars that choice will move; you let go; a one-line reply tells you what happened ("412 takes the Penthouse. The swan keeps 412."); the bars move visibly and keep their ▲▼ until your next decision; the clock ticks seven minutes. Every run (2–3 minutes, about 24 cards): one Trouble runs as a spine from the first card at 3:00 through two or three beats to the Rush at 5:20 (three or four cards with the odds spelled out, where a steady nerve makes the odds kinder), and then breakfast at 6:00 or a fall somewhere before it; a guest reviews your night out of five stars; AGAIN is one tap and under a second away. Every day: Tonight, the same Trouble and the same shuffle for everyone, one attempt that is safe to put down and pick up, a review to post, a streak of nights played, and a new page in the Guest Book.
