The game in three sentences

The world is sliding towards war. Red spreads across the map by itself, day by day. You lead the Republic, and you have until the war covers the world to get a treaty signed.

That is also the explanation at the start, three lines over the live map, skippable. The test of every screen stays: someone who never plays games, watching over the player's shoulder, can say what is happening and what the player is trying to do. Here they say: "red is spreading, she's popping the bubbles and buying things, and the blue bar has to beat the red one."

What is on the screen, top to bottom

  • The top bar. INFLUENCE (the points you spend; Plague Inc's DNA), the day, and pause / play / fast.
  • The race. Two bars with the same finish line. WAR fills red as regions fall (the finish is twenty of thirty). TREATY fills blue as blocs come aboard. Whichever reaches the end first ends the game.
  • The map. The thirty fictional regions in five blocs: the Cap (the Federation), Highmarch (the Republic, you, with the flag), the Scatter, the Sound, Farsouth. Calm, tense, fighting. Fighting spreads along borders and sea lanes. Bubbles appear on it and fade if not tapped: amber for influence, red for a flashpoint, blue for a talk.
  • The ticker. One line of headlines: what the world did, what you did, what it cost.
  • The panel. Three tabs. WORLD: the state of the map in a sentence and the legend. POLICY: the tree. TREATY: the blocs and what each wants.

Nothing else. No numbers on the map, no pins, no cards on the table. The crisis popups are the only thing that ever covers the map, and they stop the clock.

How it plays

  • Time. A game is sixty days. A day is about twelve seconds at normal speed, four at fast, so a full game is twelve to fifteen minutes. Pause whenever you like. Every popup pauses.
  • Spread. A fighting region spreads to a neighbour every few days, faster as more of the world fights. A region beside fighting turns tense first, and a tense region shows a red flashpoint bubble with a timer ring; handle it in time and it calms, miss it and it falls. Calm regions can also turn tense from the arcs and from your own deterrence moves.
  • Influence. Earned by tapping amber bubbles, by a small daily trickle from every calm region in your bloc, and by Home Front policies. Spent on the tree and on bringing blocs aboard the treaty.
  • The treaty. Starts at zero. Blue talk bubbles add a few percent each; Diplomacy policies add a little every day; each bloc that signs adds twenty. A bloc signs when its condition is met and nothing in it is fighting. The Federation signs last, and only when the fighting it started has stopped. That tension between the two bars is the game: every point of spread makes the treaty harder.
  • Win. Treaty at 100%: "The treaty was signed on day 41." The day is the score.
  • Lose. Fighting in twenty regions, or fighting in the Republic's capital: "World war, day 33." The map shows where it began and where it ended.
  • Saved after every day. CONTINUE on the title. No accounts, no network, as before.

The policy tree

Eighteen nodes, six a branch, bought in order down the branch with rising costs. Each is one line of plain English. This is the Balatro part, in Plague Inc's clothes: a build that pays off, visible on the map.

Diplomacy

  1. Hotline · a flashpoint you tap also calms its neighbours a little
  2. Back-channel · blue talk bubbles last twice as long
  3. Envoy corps · talk bubbles appear twice as often
  4. Summit · Farsouth's condition; treaty +1% a day
  5. Ceasefire talks · a fighting region can be talked down (a blue bubble appears on it)
  6. Grand bargain · every bloc aboard adds 30 instead of 20

Deterrence

  1. Alert level · the Republic's capital cannot fall while held; its neighbours turn tense
  2. Fleet posture · fighting cannot cross a sea lane to your bloc
  3. Forward brigade · spread into Highmarch halves; the Federation's regions turn tense
  4. Border guard · flashpoints on your borders tick twice as slowly
  5. Air cover · spread everywhere slows by a third for ten days
  6. Last warning · the Federation stops spreading for five days; a false alarm costs a region

Home Front

  1. Press office · amber bubbles give double
  2. Intelligence · flashpoints are announced a day early in the ticker
  3. Civil defence · a fallen region in your bloc counts half towards WAR
  4. Rationing · the daily trickle doubles
  5. Cyber shield · the cyberattack and false-alarm arcs cannot hurt you
  6. War chest · every node costs a quarter less

The phone at 3am

Ten arcs from the original brief, three to five popups each, chained over days: the satellite blackout, the false alarm, the undersea cables, the proxy incident, the cyberattack on early warning, the blockade, the defector, the debris cascade, the ultimatum, the summit. A popup stops the clock, puts one adviser's message on the screen (forty words or fewer) and two or three choices. Every choice says what it costs and what it does in words: Influence −10 · Shoal stays open · Lintel turns tense; Treaty +4% · slower; 60% it works · if not, Shoal falls. About one popup every two days. They are where the story lives and where the grip comes from; the map is where you see what you did.

What I would build first

A playable slice, nothing more: the real-time map with spread and the three kinds of bubble, the two bars, pause and fast, the ticker, the tree with all eighteen nodes, twelve popups from three arcs, win and lose, the three-line explanation. No sound, no menus beyond pause, no save. Then your partner plays it while you watch, before anything else is added. If the slice does not grip, the design is wrong and we change the design, not the polish.

Three things to decide

  1. Only bubbles, popups and the tree act on the map. As in Plague Inc, you never move a fleet or draw a line on the map by hand. I recommend this: it keeps the map a thing you watch and read. Say if you want direct map moves.
  2. Length. Sixty days at twelve to fifteen minutes a game. Plague Inc runs twenty to thirty. I recommend the shorter game for a phone; say if you want it longer.
  3. The look. The screens above are the war-room dressing you chose: near-black panels, thin rules, status-lamp colours, condensed capitals, a dark slate map with red hatching. If anything here is wrong, name it now; it is cheaper to change in a picture than in a build.

All places, powers and people are fictional. BRINK is a working title kept in one constant. No accounts, no chat, no user content; nothing leaves the phone.
