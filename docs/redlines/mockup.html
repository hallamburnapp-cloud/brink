Treaty against war: the other side thinks

The first draft was reviewed and found thin: a firefighter against weather, a tree that solves itself in three runs, a treaty you could sign while the world burned. This draft keeps what held up (the two-bar race, the real-time map, plain-word costs, the slice first) and rebuilds the middle. The Federation has a mind of its own, your promises deter only when you are believed, the cabinet is drafted, not climbed. Three independent critics then tore into this draft before you saw it, and their fixes are in. Below: the screen, then the game, then what both reviews said and what changed.

The game in three sentences

The Federation is pushing. Where it pushes, the map turns amber, then red.
You lead the Republic. Work out what kind of enemy this is, promise to defend what you can hold, and keep your word when they test you.
Give them a way to stop and they sign. Let them win, or back them into a corner, and it is war.

That is also the explanation at the start, over the live map, skippable, with THE FEDERATION lettered across the Cap and a flag on each capital, and a third line that names this run's dilemma ("You inherited a promise to Shoal. We think they will not stand for a fleet there."). The test of every screen stays: someone who never plays games, watching over the player's shoulder, can say what is happening and what the player is trying to do. Here: "the Federation, the flag up top, keeps pushing; she's working out what they're like and promising to defend places; the blue bar is them agreeing to stop."

The other side

Every Federation is drawn with two hidden dials, appetite (how hard it pushes unguarded places) and jumpiness (how little force near its borders it tolerates). The stronger dial names what they're like, but the other is always there, so the right amount of firmness is found by feel, not by reading a label. Intelligence never names them; it describes what they have done, and the ending names them. Once a run, on a trigger, they can change.

  • Every brake buys days, not the game. It drops them one step for five to eight days; then they resume against a new target and the same brake costs more. A region taken unanswered is a step up for every Federation: gains breed pushes.
  • Their lines are declared, and some are bluffs. Two or three real red lines and one or two bluffs per run, each announced before it can be crossed ("Wintermast: Republic forces in the Sound would be a grave matter"). Intelligence says "they say the Sound is a line; 40 to 60% they mean it". Crossing a bluff costs nothing and makes them believe you more. Crossing a real one climbs the ladder within a day, with the headline that says why. A pullback uncrosses it at a small cost.
  • The ladder is on the screen, under the two bars, at all times: they are TESTING, PUSHING, MASSING, FIGHTING or ALL-OUT. Up a step: a real line crossed, a promise backed down from, a gain unanswered, their own accelerator, an accident. Down: their brake, a way out accepted, two pushes blocked in a row. At MASSING and FIGHTING both sides are afraid: the treaty rises every day, but every day carries a small chance of an accidental step up. Climbing to force a settlement is possible, and dangerous. A Federation left FIGHTING for ten days climbs on its own, so the clock cannot be run out.
  • What they think of you is hidden and spoken: "They think you'll back down. We are fairly sure." They believe you more when you back a promise and when you do more than you say; less when you back down in public or a bluff of yours is called.
  • The blue bar is intelligence's estimate of their willingness to sign, two or three days behind the truth with a soft edge, so it cannot be used as an instant probe. It rises on quiet days only while they are TESTING or PUSHING; above that, only blocks, ways out and other blocs' signatures move it, so sitting still is not a strategy. It is capped at 75% until they have a way out, and they sign only when no region has fallen for seven days.

The world and your promises

  • The map. Thirty regions, five blocs. Highmarch is the Republic, you. The Cap is the Federation. The Scatter and the Sound are the ground between you. Farsouth is the Coalition, a swing power whose bloc tints towards whichever side it leans; its signature is worth the most, and once courted it acts once per run on its own, attributed to you, which can cross "no alliance with Farsouth" on your behalf. Alliance has a price.
  • Three states, and regions recover. Calm. Under pressure (amber, dotted, the dots thickening as it nears falling). Fighting (red, hatched). Nothing spreads by itself. A fighting region drops back to under pressure when the ladder steps down or a way out names it; under pressure returns to calm after seven quiet days. The WAR bar runs both ways.
  • What is drawn. Your promises as white outlines marked WE DEFEND. Your deployments as a chevron (land) or an anchor (sea). Their forces as markers moving towards the regions they will push next, two days ahead. A ring where you are being tested, left on the map for the day. Nothing to tap but the regions themselves and the tabs. No bubbles.
  • Promises are the main verb. Tap and hold a region outside the Cap: PROMISE TO DEFEND. It costs one influence a day; at most three; you can withdraw one quietly before it is tested (they believe you a little less, far less than a back-down). A promise deters when you are believed: at good standing a promised region is pushed half as often and falls at half speed; at poor standing it invites a test. The confirm says which. Every push on a promised region is a test, and the phone rings the day it turns amber. Defending without a promise is possible through a FORCE card, costs more, and the Coalition reads it as overreach. You inherit nought to two promises, and the setup guarantees that one of them, or one of their claims, collides with a probable red line: that is the dilemma the run opens on.

The cabinet, drafted

Five drafts, on days 1, 12, 24, 36 and 48: three cards from a pool of forty; take one, none for +6 influence, or reroll once for 3. Hand of six; measures are spent when played, people and policies stay. Tags FORCE, TALK, INTEL, HOME, with base synergies by tag so every run has some (two FORCE: backing costs less; TALK + INTEL: the range on a way out narrows; two HOME: the income floor rises) and named pairs as rare spikes. Every FORCE card prints its risk, every TALK card its cost, every INTEL card its chance of being wrong (the Spymaster is wrong one time in six and says so; Intercepts can be caught). No card removes content and no card removes the doubt. Measures are played only from a phone, as extra options when the card is in hand, so the clock never runs while you act.

Influence: start 30, +2 a day, −1 a day per promise; backing a promise costs 12 the first time and falls to 6 once they believe you, so the record of keeping your word pays. The cost to back a promise is printed on its outline the day it is tested, and the Chief of Staff warns the day before you would be short.

The phone at 3am

About eight scheduled calls a game, one a week, plus a test whenever they push a promised region. A test is up to three rounds with the clock stopped: back it, back down, or wait, each with its cost in words; if you back it and they do not move, a second round ("They have not moved. Reinforce / Hold / Pull back"). People in your cabinet add a line each. There are two kinds of line (a private warning through the Back-channel, or a public promise) and two kinds of block (loud, with the Press Secretary, which humiliates them; quiet, which lets them climb down). The Intel Director speaks in ranges about events and in words about states, and a validator checks that her ranges are honest. The false alarm is tied to the ladder: below ALL-OUT a "real" alarm is a test or an exercise, never an instant loss; the cost is in how you respond. Ways out name their content (a promise you drop, a claim you concede, a deployment you pull back, a summit) and print the read and the price. About thirteen stops in a median game, one every forty seconds of running clock; the next call's day is in the top bar.

Why you play again

  • Every run is set up differently: the two dials and the name, which lines are real and which are bluffs, the claims, the promises you inherit, the Coalition's lean, the first flashpoints, the shuffle, and a guaranteed collision between a promise and a line.
  • Three leaders, one unlock per win in a fixed order: the Dove (Hotline in hand, ways out cheap), the Hawk (the first backing of the run is free), the Spymaster (one of their lines known on day one; believed less at the start).
  • A daily seed: the same world for everyone on a date; your best stays on your phone. Nothing is shared, nobody is named.

Endings and the score

  • Treaty. "Signed on day 41 with two regions still fighting. They stopped because they could not take Vessary and you gave them a way out." The ending names what they were like.
  • World war. "Day 33. It began at Shoal, when the promise you inherited was tested and you backed down." The map draws the chain through your deployments, their lines and the steps.
  • Frozen. Day 60 with neither. "Nothing was signed. Two regions still fight. They came within a week of stopping." Below every treaty.
  • One number: (60 − day) × 10, less 15 per region fighting at the signing.

What is built first

A slice: the Federation with two names and per-run dials; declared lines with bluffs; the ladder with its fear and its accidents; promises that deter when believed; tests of up to three rounds; a way out with named content; regions that recover; the ladder word and your deployments on screen; one inherited-promise template; twenty of the forty cards with the five drafts; eight phone templates from three arcs; the three endings; the explanation with the collision named. Left out by decision, because they are research projects in disguise: a general misperception model, domestic politics beyond one dial, intelligence that infers rather than counts, four fully distinct behaviours with learned tells, leaders, the daily seed, sound, save. The simulator passes its gates before anyone plays; then five cold testers, three games each.

Four things to decide

  1. Fog at the start. Intelligence never names what they're like, only describes; their lines are declared but "they mean it" starts at 40 to 60%. More fog is more real and harder to read over a shoulder.
  2. Stops. Five drafts and about eight scheduled calls, plus tests: about thirteen in a median game. Fewer, and the drafts fold into the calls.
  3. The Coalition as the swing power, with one action of its own per run, or five equal blocs.
  4. Length. Sixty days (about twenty minutes a full game) or forty-five (about fifteen).

All places, powers and people are fictional. BRINK is a working title kept in one constant. No accounts, no chat, no user content; nothing leaves the phone. The full design is in the repository at docs/redlines/SPEC.md, with your review and the three critics' findings beside it.
