Pick a look. Then the small game.

Your second review said the design now rests on a real idea but had grown too dense, that the clock was dead weight, that the phone handed the player the answer, and that the look was a dashboard, not a game. Below, first, the same moment (day 18, and the 03:14 call) drawn three ways by three independent art directors, so the look is chosen before anything is built. Then the design cut down to the small game the review asked for: one Federation, promises you can or cannot hold, one phone with advisers who argue in character, two bars, eight weeks, and a debrief that lifts the fog at the end.

A · The situation room

A paper situation map under a desk lamp: ink coastlines with water-lining and hachured hills on warm grained paper, bloc washes hand-tinted, names set like a printed sheet, and on top of it the marks of a person losing sleep — black grease-pencil rings for the promises with a scrawled WE DEFEND, red pencil hatching where the Federation leans, a red wash and FIGHTING stamp on Driftholm, red pushpins with a pencil arrow for the columns on the move, and Lintel over-circled in hard red pencil with TESTED underlined; the two bars are pencil hatching on an index card paper-clipped to the map's corner, the ladder word is a red rubber stamp, the news is a strip of teleprinter tape. The call is the clock stopped on a dark flip-tag at 03:14, a bakelite handset lying off the hook across the map with its coiled cord trailing off the desk, General Halder's words typed on a clipped card, three torn adviser slips, and the three choices as typed forms with a pencil underline and a handwritten 1, 2, 3.

B · Vector minimalism

A black screen with one glowing world on it: thin pale coastlines with a soft halo, land as faint fills, a dim wide-tracked PUSHING lying in the strait the Federation is crossing, and the danger painted so it reads from across the room: amber hatching sweeping down the eastern shore, Driftholm a pulsing red knot, your three promises in crisp white with WE DEFEND, and a white target reticle breathing on Lintel. Colour means one thing each (cyan is the Federation and its treaty bar, white is you, amber is pressure, red is fighting, grey is the world and its chrome), and the only chrome is two thin lines at the top, one ticker line at the bottom, and two small readouts in the corners; the call is a vector panel hung from the reticle by a leader line, with a line-drawn General Halder, his forty words, three advisers in character, and three outlined buttons of which only BACK IT glows white.

The game in three sentences

  1. The Federation is pushing. Where it pushes, the map turns amber, then red.
  2. You lead the Republic. Promise to defend what you can hold, and keep your word when they test you.
  3. Give them a way to stop and they sign. Let them win, or back them into a corner, and it is war.

A week (one turn)

  1. See. The map shows what they did last week and where their forces are heading now (two places; one is real, one may be a feint). The headline strip says what changed and why, in one line each.
  2. Act. Draw or withdraw promises (at most three; solid where you can hold them, dashed where you cannot). Move your two pieces (one fleet, one brigade): a piece within reach makes a promise solid. Offer a way out if you want to (one of three named terms: drop a promise, pull a piece back, a summit without preconditions; each with its price). Each act costs influence; you have about 30 and earn about 8 a week.
  3. The phone. If they tested a promise this week, or an event fired, the call: the caller's message in forty words, the advisers in character, then BACK IT / BACK DOWN / WAIT. WAIT carries the test into next week with the stakes up.
  4. Resolve. Pushes land, places fall or recover, the ladder moves, the two bars move, and the strip says why.

Eight weeks. A full game is ten minutes.

The other side

One Federation per run, drawn with two hidden dials: appetite (how hard it pushes unguarded places) and

jumpiness (how little force near its borders it tolerates). No names, no profiles on screen; the debrief

names them at the end. It declares three lines before the game starts (in the opening strip): two real, one a

bluff; you are not told which. Crossing a real line moves it up the ladder; crossing the bluff does nothing and

makes it believe you more. Its ladder is one word on screen: TESTING, PUSHING, MASSING, FIGHTING, ALL-OUT. Up a

step: a real line crossed, a promise you backed down from, a place taken unanswered, or, at MASSING and above,

an accident (one chance in twenty a week). Down a step: a promise backed and held, a way out accepted. It

believes you more when you back a promise you could hold; less when you back down, or back a promise you could

not hold and it fails.

It tests promises it does not believe. That is the whole deterrence model, and it is visible: dashed

outlines get tested; solid ones mostly do not.

The two bars

  • WAR: regions fighting, drawn as twelve ticks; the twelfth is WORLD WAR and the game is lost there.
  • TREATY: their willingness to sign, 0 to 100. Each week: +5 if they gained nothing this week; +15 when a

    way out is accepted; +10 when another bloc signs (at most two); −5 for each place they gained; −10 when they

    are humiliated (a public ultimatum they must refuse). At 100 they sign, if no place fell this week. Those

    are all the rules the bar has.

Endings and the score

  • TREATY on week w with f places fighting: score 100 − 10w − 5f, never below 10. "Signed in week 6 with one

    place still fighting. They stopped because they could not take Vessary and you gave them a way out."

  • FROZEN: week 8 ends with neither. Score 9 − f, never below 1. "Nothing was signed."
  • WORLD WAR: score 0. "Week 5. It began at Shoal, when the promise you inherited was tested and you backed

    down." The map draws the chain.

  • Then the debrief: the eight weeks replayed with their hidden thoughts shown at each decision, and the two

    dials named in words ("They were hungry and not easily scared").

The screen (whichever look the owner picks)

The map is the hero and takes most of the screen. Above it, small: the week, the influence, the two bars and the

ladder word. Below it, one line of headline. Promises as outlines (solid or dashed) marked WE DEFEND; your two

pieces drawn; their forces drawn moving; the tested place marked. The call covers the map and stops everything:

a face, forty words, three choices. Nothing else. No tabs in the first build; the sentence a watcher needs is

the headline line.

What is built first, this week

The map with its states; one Federation with two dials and three declared lines; promises with hold and bluff;

two pieces; the ladder; the two bars; eight weeks; the phone with eight templates (two inherited-promise tests,

two feints, a false alarm, a trawler incident, a bluff crossed, a summit offer); the three endings; the debrief.

No cabinet, no Coalition, no leaders, no daily seed, no sound, no save. A simulator with four bots (passive,

promise-everything, far-promise opening, sensible) runs before anyone plays: the passive bot must lose most

games, the far-promise opening must not beat the sensible bot, and the sensible bot must win between a third

and a half. Then five cold testers who are not the owner's partner, two games each and the debrief; the pass is

that most of them can say what the Federation was like without being told, and play a third game unasked.

Two decisions for the owner

  1. Turn-based by week, or real time. The review's case for turns is strong (nothing happens between stops that needs a clock). The owner chose real time for the Plague Inc feel; this page recommends turns.
  2. Who it is for. A broad phone game (this cut) or a deep strategy game for a niche (then v5.1's cabinet, Coalition and warnings come back in later builds, but not in the first).

All places, powers and people are fictional. BRINK is a working title kept in one constant. No accounts, no chat, no user content; nothing leaves the phone.
