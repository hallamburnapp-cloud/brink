# Review of the first redesign draft ("Treaty Against War"), as received from the owner

Kept verbatim because the second draft (`SPEC.md`) answers it point by point.

---

**Verdict: as designed, BRINK isn't addictive. It's a thin, defensive version of a game that already exists. It would hold someone for two or three runs and then be solved.** The playable-slice plan is the best thing on the page. Most of the design above that plan needs rethinking.

## 1. It's Rebel Inc, minus the depth

The pitch is "Plague Inc's shape, inverted": you contain the red instead of spreading it. Ndemic already shipped that game (Rebel Inc; Plague Inc's Cure mode). Plague Inc works because growth is your power fantasy. Invert it and the player becomes a firefighter; that can work only if putting fires out involves real choices. Here it mostly involves reflexes.

## 2. The main verb is tapping bubbles, the weakest part of Plague Inc

Three bubble types on a 390×520 map with 30 regions. The verb that matters most needs the least thought and contradicts "keep the map a thing you watch and read". Colour collisions: amber bubbles over amber regions, red flashpoints over red regions; red versus amber is the worst pair for colour-blind players.

## 3. The treaty maths breaks the core story

Five blocs at 20% each; Highmarch signs on day 1, so you start at 20%. Talk bubbles plus Summit's +1% a day supply 20% over 40 days. So you can win without the Federation ever signing; with Grand Bargain four blocs make 120%. A treaty signed while 19 of 30 regions burn counts as victory.

## 4. The policy tree is not "the Balatro part"

Balatro's grip is random offers, combinatorial synergies, runs that differ. Three fixed linear tracks converge on one buy order within three plays. Alert Level is a dominant first buy. Deterrence's trade-off is claimed, not designed (Fleet Posture, Border Guard, Air Cover have no downside). War Chest comes last where it is worth least. Half the tree amplifies bubbles. Cyber Shield deletes two of ten arcs. Intelligence announces things in the ticker, the one element you can't watch while tapping.

## 5. Content runs out on run two

About 30 popups a game from a pool of 30–50. One game shows half to all of the content. A popup every ~24 s at normal speed, ~8 s on fast: the fast button means nothing and a real-time game that halts that often is turn-based with friction.

## 6. No variance, no meta-progression, so no "one more run"

One map, one nation, one adversary, one fixed tree, fixed arcs, a single score. Income from calm regions in your bloc is a death spiral with no comeback.

## 7. It throws away your actual edge

War is modelled as contagion by adjacency; "deterrence" is a firewall; the Federation is weather, not an opponent. The things that make escalation interesting (adversaries who respond to signals, credibility, commitment traps, alliance obligations, misperception) are left out. Popups showing exact outcomes remove the fog; the false-alarm arc tells you precisely what is real. The open lane is a crisis game where the other side thinks.

## What's genuinely good

The two-bar race. Short sessions. Costs stated in plain words. A fictional world (though 30 invented names carry no emotional weight). The slice-first rule, tested with more than one player.

## What I'd change

1. Make the Federation an agent: hidden doctrine, red lines, an escalation ladder that reacts to your signals.
2. Add fog: confidence ranges, ambiguous false alarms, consequences that land days later.
3. Draft, don't climb: three random cards a week from a pool of about 40 with synergies.
4. Randomise setup: aggressor, alliance commitments, starting flashpoints, hidden red lines; a daily seed.
5. Leader types that change the opening (Hawk, Dove, Spymaster), unlocked by winning.
6. Fix the win condition: all five signatures, or the Federation's with fighting below a threshold.
7. Fewer, heavier popups, about one a week, from a large recombinant pool; influence through decisions; cut the amber bubbles.

## What would change the verdict

Cold testers replaying five or more times unprompted; a classroom goal rather than a commercial one; a far larger, more recombinant popup pool.
