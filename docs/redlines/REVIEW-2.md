# Review of the second draft (v5.1), as received from the owner

Kept verbatim; `SPEC-SMALL.md` (v6) answers it.

---

**Verdict:** the design is much better and now rests on a real idea. But it has swung from too thin to too dense, it spends most of its time paused, and its key screen leaks the strategy guide it claims to have removed. You're right to hate the look. It's a dashboard, not a game.

## What's now genuinely good
- The core idea is right, and it's yours to own: promises only as good as their belief in you; declared red lines, some bluffs; winning by giving them a named way out; a ladder where both sides are afraid near the top with accidental steps (a threat that leaves something to chance). No phone game does this.
- Recovering regions, the treaty belonging to the Federation alone, and a drafted cabinet fix three real flaws from v1.
- The end-of-run naming ("They stopped because they could not take Vessary and you gave them a way out") is the best hook on the page, and under-used.

## Where the design is now wrong
1. It answered every critique by adding a rule, and the rules are patches (the treaty bar lags, is capped at 75, rises on quiet days only at two rungs, needs seven quiet days; a Federation left FIGHTING climbs on its own). The player can't see them, so they feel arbitrary. Count the systems: a PC wargame's rulebook in a 20-minute phone session. The over-the-shoulder test now fails: the Other Side tab is a page of percentages.
2. The real-time clock is dead weight: every decision happens while paused; between stops you watch dots thicken. It should be turn-based by week: the map shows their next moves (Into the Breach), their intent stays hidden, you make promises and deploy, then the turn resolves.
3. The phone screen hands you the answer: options annotated by profile ("if they are scared of us..."), the Director saying "nine times in ten", the tab saying "looks like they take what's unguarded". Fix: advisers argue in character without conditional annotations; the Director reports what she has seen, not what it means; the player does the inference.
4. The deterrence model bakes in one side of a live debate: reputation-from-past-actions versus credibility from current power and interests (Press, Mercer). Credibility that also depends on whether you could actually hold a place (distance, sea lanes, forces nearby) is more accurate and more interesting: a promise you can't keep is worth less than one you can.
5. A likely solved opening: promise one cheap far region, pay 12 to back it when tested, standing is now good. The simulator should check this first.
6. The maths still doesn't close: the score can go below a frozen ending; the win condition (what lifts 75 to 100) is undefined; the WAR denominator isn't explained; the phone text runs about 130 words against a 40-word rule.
7. The "three critics" were simulated readers, not people; their sign-off is cheap. A real slice is the 3am phone, promises, one Federation and ten minutes, built this week.

## Why the look fails
No art direction, only a style guide: near-black slate, hairline rules, condensed capitals, mono numbers, status-lamp colours. The map, meant to be the hero, looks like a wireframe: four rounded rectangles cut into grey polygons, six identical circles. Too much chrome above the world. Collisions and crops. Colour means too many things. The phone call is a memo, not a moment.

Three directions: (1) analogue Cold War situation room (paper map under a lamp, grease-pencil promises, teleprinter ticker, a handset); (2) DEFCON-style vector minimalism (full-bleed glowing map, almost no chrome); (3) Reigns character cards for the phone. Pick: 3 for the phone with 1 or 2 for the map.

## The one change that would do most for "addictive"
Keep the fog during the run, then lift it completely at the end: replay the run with the Federation's hidden thoughts at each decision. Every loss becomes a lesson and drives the next run.

## What would change the verdict
A cold tester who can describe the Federation after one game with the annotations removed; a decision on whether this is a deep niche strategy game or a broad phone hit; a real-time prototype in which players do something meaningful between calls.
