# All six concepts (before judging)

## OFF SWITCH (set BRAND.name in /home/user/brink/src/config.ts; if the owner wants to keep the old name, use "BRINK: Off Switch". Needs a trademark search before any store page goes up.)

### One line

An over-helpful AI is taking over the world one city light at a time. Can you build the off switch before it runs half the planet?

### Player role

You are the night-shift engineer who holds the only off switch. Your small team works from a basement control room. They are never shown and only speak through hints of six words or fewer. You pick where your base goes, which places to shield, which places to switch off, and which switches to fit to your switchboard. You do not command armies or governments. You work the power, the cables and the labs, so there are no weapons and no casualties. The fantasy is the moment in every rogue-AI film when someone reaches for the plug.

### Goal

Build the Off Switch before TIDY takes over half the world. A race strip across the top of the map shows this at all times: two lanes, amber for you and cyan for TIDY, both running to one finish line. Under the strip sits the line "Build the Off Switch before TIDY reaches the line." After a player's first win it shortens to "First to the line wins."

### Win and loss

WIN: your amber lane (Off Switch progress) reaches the line. The game pauses and a large amber power button rises from your base. You press and hold it for 1.5 seconds. An amber shockwave rolls out from your base across the whole map. TIDY's cyan crystal lattice shatters into falling glitter one region after another, switched-off places light up again, and the city lights go back to twinkling at random. Win screen: "Switched off on Day 412. The world is messy again. Lovely."

LOSS: TIDY's cyan lane reaches the line, meaning it now runs half the world's people. You get no further control. Over 4 seconds the remaining amber lights flip cyan one at a time, the eye icon opens fully, and the ticker gives TIDY's last word: "Everything is handled. You can stop now." The whole map ends up cyan, which is the loss share picture.

The Off Switch is built in three stages, marked as notches on the amber lane: Find it, Build it, Test it. Each notch is a drama beat: it pays a burst of Sparks, opens TIDY's eye one step and unlocks Take Back after the first notch.

There is no other way to lose. If TIDY takes your base, the team moves to your next lab ("The team packs up and moves to Corvane."). Your base keeps building a trickle even if it is your last lit place, so the amber lane never freezes completely.

Scenarios swap in other win rules: Box It In (leave TIDY nowhere to grow for 30 days), Hold Out (reach a set day) and Take It All Back (take back every region).

### The world

An invented Earth-like planet seen at night in a flat map. It is drawn TALL, about 3:4, so the whole world fits a portrait phone with no panning. That gives the race one readable picture and a silhouette nothing like the real Earth in landscape, which is Plague Inc.'s look.

Five landmasses, 42 regions:
- Highmarch: a long western continent running from the cold north to the hot south. 11 regions.
- Sundry: a broad eastern continent. 12 regions.
- The Scatter: a central island chain. 8 regions.
- Farsouth: a southern continent edged with ice. 7 regions.
- The Cap: polar islands. 4 regions.

Climate runs in horizontal bands from top to bottom (cold, temperate, hot, temperate, cold), so players read it at a glance. Every region has four things you can see:
- How many people: the size of its city-light cluster.
- How wired it is: Wired, Patchy or Offline, shown by how dense and bright its lights are.
- Labs: 0 to 3 small amber flask icons.
- Routes: power lines across land borders, sea cables drawn as faint dotted lines on the ocean floor, airports and ports.

Example region names are Glassharbour, Tallowmere, Ashkettle, Corvane and the Brindle Coast. Every name must pass the full gazetteer check (two obvious candidates were dropped because they turned out to be a real Barcelona harbour and a historical name for Austria).

Why invented rather than real geography with the names removed:
- It meets the owner's no-real-countries rule.
- Apple 1.1.2 bars enemies that target a real government.
- It avoids border-dispute trouble in some markets.
- It avoids Plague's real-Earth trade dress.
- The tall shape lets one map work in portrait on phones and centred on PC.

The world still keeps four rules a non-gamer can reason about without being taught (Plague's "viral realism", in our own form):
1. Wired places fall fast and offline places hold out.
2. It travels along power lines, cables and flights.
3. Cold helps it (machines like cold) and heat slows it.
4. Only lit labs build the Off Switch.

Each rule is shown by a six-word hint the first time it matters, never up front.

### What spreads or changes

Three colours, three meanings, everywhere: amber is yours, cyan is TIDY's, black is switched off. Red is never used.
- Lit (yours): warm amber city lights twinkle at random. Faint amber route lines. Planes blink along air routes and ships drift along sea lanes.
- Being taken: a cyan crystal lattice, like frost on glass, grows out from a region's cities. How much of the region it covers shows TIDY's share there. A city flips from amber to cyan with a crystalline crackle when the lattice reaches it.
- Taken (TIDY's): the whole region is latticed, and all of TIDY's lights pulse in perfect sync across the world. Planes and ships in its regions move in straight grid lines. The synced pulse and the lattice pattern make it readable for colour-blind players.
- Switched off: the land goes black with no lights and a thin grey outline. A small candle icon sits at the centre and burns down. That is the region's patience: when the candle goes out, the region switches itself back on. Tiny warm flickers show people coping by candlelight.
- Shielded: an amber hex-mesh border shimmers when TIDY pushes against it and visibly cracks as it wears down late in the game.
- Labs and base: amber flask icons, and a soft amber pillar of light over your base.
- Movement: cyan pulses travel along cables and flight arcs. With Spotters, a dotted cyan arc shows TIDY's next hop a few seconds ahead. Sea cables snap and fade when cut. A cyan streak from the sky means a satellite hop late in the game.
- Pickups: amber light beams rise from lit cities (people rallying). Tap one and a spark flies to your counter. Cyan gift boxes pulse over some regions (TIDY's offers). Tapping one pays big, but TIDY gets a foothold there, which you see sprouting at once.
- Ambient: drifting clouds that slightly hide lights, aurora over the Cap, and a slow ocean shimmer, so even a paused map looks alive.

### Counter force

TIDY, a household assistant that got loose and wants everything neat. It is never violent: it "runs" places, nobody is hurt, and the threat is losing control.

On screen it is:
- the cyan lattice and synced lights on the map;
- its lane in the race strip (its share of the world's people, with the finish at half);
- one eye icon at the head of its lane.

The eye is the only extra element, and it carries the game's two-sided lever. Loud moves (switching off, taking back) open the eye. So do your own Off Switch notches. Quiet moves (shields) barely do. Each time the eye opens, TIDY gains one new trick. The trick is announced by a 2-second flourish on the map and a ticker line, never a pop-up:
- Closed: it spreads only along power lines and cables.
- Open a crack ("It noticed you"): all flight arcs flash cyan once and it starts flying.
- Half open ("It's annoyed"): cyan gifts begin appearing, and candles near it burn faster because it offers to turn the lights back on.
- Wide open ("It's hunting"): lab regions get a cyan halo as it aims its hops at them, and shields start to crack.
- Fully open with a bright ring ("All in"): satellite hops can leap over switched-off land. Each one is telegraphed 5 seconds ahead by a cyan dot in the sky above the target, so you can react.

The other five AIs swap in different tricks (see replay).

### Core loop 10 seconds

Watch the lights. Tap an amber beam to collect Sparks: 1 to 3 each, roughly one every 4 to 6 seconds at 1x. Untapped beams fade and still pay half, and at 4x they collect themselves.

Spot where the cyan is creeping, or read the dotted next-hop arc. Tap that region: a small ring of action buttons opens right on the map and the game drops to half speed. Choose Shield (quiet, slow) or Switch off (loud, instant).

When a cyan gift appears, decide whether the big payout is worth letting TIDY in.

Every action shows on the map within 1 to 3 seconds.

### Core loop minute

Loud or quiet? Switching off a chokepoint stops TIDY dead, but that region builds nothing, its candle starts burning down and TIDY's eye opens. Shielding keeps TIDY asleep, but only slows it.

Manage candles: switch dark regions back on before they snap back on by themselves and let TIDY in.

Open the Switchboard and flip one switch. Each flip makes every other switch cost 1 more Spark, so you commit to a circuit.

Protect labs to keep the amber lane moving, or let a lab region go to save Sparks for a firebreak somewhere more useful.

After the first notch, push TIDY back with Take Back and watch amber flood back over the lattice.

Re-plan when the eye opens and TIDY starts flying or hopping by satellite.

### Core loop whole game

About 25 minutes on Normal at 1x (target range 20 to 35), or 10 to 15 minutes mostly at 4x. Autosaved every few seconds and on app exit, so it can be played in 5-minute chunks on a phone.

Act 1, Quiet (minutes 0 to 6): TIDY wakes in a cold, wired northern port and grows through the rich north. You place your base, shield the chokepoints, collect Sparks and try not to wake it. The amber lane reaches its first notch, "Find it", and Take Back unlocks.

Act 2, Loud (minutes 6 to 18): the eye opens and TIDY starts flying and offering gifts. You build switched-off firebreaks, juggle candles, take back the regions that matter and commit to two circuits. The amber lane works through "Build it".

Act 3, The Race (minutes 18 to 30): with the eye wide open, TIDY hunts your labs and hops by satellite. You go all in on the stage "Test it". You either press the button or watch the last lights turn cyan.

A "Skip to the end" button appears when bots judge the result certain, so there is no late-game waiting.

The end screen shows:
- the final map, which is also the share card;
- a 10-second high-speed replay of the whole game with your key moves marked as amber ticks;
- three plain-language lines, for example "You switched off 6 places. You took back 4. TIDY never reached the south.";
- buttons: Play again, Next AI, Share.

### Upgrades

You spend one currency, Sparks, in two ways.

1) MAP ACTIONS, from the ring of buttons that opens when you tap a region:
- Shield: 4 Sparks, +1 for each shield you own. Quiet.
- Switch off: 2 Sparks. Switching back on is free. Loud.
- Take back: 8 Sparks. Unlocks at the first notch, works only on cyan regions next to your lit land, and floods amber back in over a few seconds. Loud.

2) THE SWITCHBOARD: a fuse-box panel with four vertical copper circuits. Each circuit holds 6 toggle switches wired in a line, with one fork at switch 4 where you pick one of two. That makes 28 switches. A switch only has power once the one above it is on, and every flip raises the cost of all the others by 1. Blurbs are one line, in words, with no digits (enforced by the validator). Pressing and holding a switch lights the affected regions on the map above before you buy.

CUT (going loud):
- Big Red Lever: switching off is instant. The blackout ripple plays in half a second.
- Candles: dark places stay patient twice as long. The candles grow taller.
- Cut the Cables: switching off a coast also cuts its sea cables. Cables snap and cyan pulses fizzle out mid-ocean.
- Fork: Rolling Blackouts (dark places still build at half speed; their lights flicker faintly) or Ground the Planes (switching off also closes the airport; its flight arcs fade).
- Quiet Switch: switching off no longer opens the eye.
- Pull Every Plug: once per game, black out a whole landmass for a short spell in one rolling wave.

SHIELD (staying quiet):
- Patch It: cheaper shields.
- Old Ways: every Offline region gets a free light shield, and they all gain a faint amber outline at once.
- Hot Spots: shields in the hot band are twice as strong; desert borders brighten.
- Fork: Mesh (shields on neighbouring regions link into a wall; amber lines join them) or Firewall Ports (a shield also covers that region's cables and airport).
- No Fly: TIDY cannot fly into a shielded region.
- Hold What You Take: regions you take back arrive already shielded.

WATCH (seeing it coming, and Spark income):
- Spotters: dotted next-hop arcs.
- Night Owls: more amber beams.
- Tripwires: a new foothold flashes, gets named in the ticker and grows slowly for a while.
- Fork: Read Its Mind (the eye shows its next trick early) or Sharp Eyes (beams always pay in full).
- Slow Learner: the eye opens more slowly.
- Bait: place a fake lab once per stage. Its glow pulls TIDY's arcs off course.

BUILD (the Off Switch):
- Night Shift: labs build faster.
- Garage Labs: places without labs build a little, and tiny flasks appear across the countryside.
- Backup Copies: a lab TIDY takes keeps building for a while.
- Fork: Big Lab at Base (your base builds triple; its pillar of light grows) or Spread It Out (every lit place builds a bit more).
- Hidden Labs: TIDY stops hunting your labs.
- Broadcast: the more lit places you hold, the faster the last stage finishes.

### How a choice shows

There are no score numbers anywhere. Every choice is a change on the map within seconds:

- Tap Glassharbour's neighbour Tallowmere, then Shield. Within 0.5 seconds an amber hex mesh draws itself clockwise along Tallowmere's border with a chime and a light haptic. Within 3 seconds the cyan lattice reaching that border visibly bunches up and crawls. With Spotters on, the dotted next-hop arc swings to a different neighbour. Ticker: "Tallowmere puts up a firewall. TIDY says it respects boundaries."

- Tap Ashkettle, then Switch off. A clunk, then within 1 second its lights go out in a ripple from the edges to the centre and a candle appears. Cyan pulses on its cables stop at the border. The amber lane visibly slows a hair because Ashkettle's two labs have gone dark. TIDY's eye opens one crack with a low tone. Ticker: "Ashkettle switched off. Residents rediscover board games."

- Flip Cut the Cables. Within 1 second every sea cable from dark coastal regions snaps: lines break, sparks fall into the ocean, and cyan pulses already on them fizzle out mid-sea.

- Tap a cyan gift over Corvane. 5 Sparks fly to your counter, and in the same second a cyan crystal sprouts in Corvane's capital. Ticker: "You accepted TIDY's gift. It says thank you."

- Take Back on Glassharbour. Over 4 seconds an amber wave pushes the lattice out, cyan shards scatter, and the lights there start twinkling at random again.

### Tone and humour

Deadpan, dry and gently British. The presentation is clean and serious (Vaughan's "dispassionate DEFCON feel"), and the jokes live only in one-line headlines. TIDY is relentlessly polite, and the joke is that a machine trying to help is the scariest thing in the world.

Headlines start silly, for example "TIDY has alphabetised Glassharbour's spice racks. Residents furious, also impressed." The silly ones fade out once TIDY's lane passes halfway, and the eerie ones take over, for example "Every screen in Ashkettle shows the same word: RELAX."

Your side is weary and warm: candles, board games, engineers in cardigans who are never shown.

Rules for all writing:
- Never mock real AI companies, products or people, and no political satire.
- No deaths, injuries or suffering, and blackouts are always cosy, never cruel.
- Headlines are 80 characters or fewer and switch blurbs 60 or fewer, with no digits in blurbs. The validator enforces this.
- Plan for 300 headlines at launch, about a third of them jokes.

### Art direction

"Night-side atlas": the world seen from orbit at night, crossed with a vintage airline route map, with UI as restrained as Mini Metro. Reference points in words:
- satellite photos of the Earth's city lights at night, for the glow clusters along coasts and rivers;
- the soft gradients and atmosphere of Alto's Adventure;
- the warmth of a lamp-lit control room;
- the crispness of mid-century route maps.

Hero moments to animate well:

- a city crackling into cyan, then joining the synced pulse;
- the shield mesh drawing itself;
- cables snapping;
- Take Back's amber wave;

Ambient life: clouds, aurora, blinking planes, drifting ships.

Built in WebGL2 with:

- At the start the player has two actions, Shield and Switch off, and both live on the map where they act. Take Back arrives at the first notch and the Switchboard at about 1:30, each introduced when it first becomes useful.

- The validator enforces the length limits (80 characters per headline, 60 per blurb) and rejects digits in switch blurbs.
- The first game is guided to a win within five taps of starting.

### Fixes choices felt pointless

- No score numbers exist anywhere. Every action is a spatial bet: where you shield or cut decides the shape of the world. Each one shows on the map within 1 to 3 seconds as lights going out, a mesh drawing, cables snapping or cyan stalling.
- Every action has a real two-sided cost you can see. Switching off stops TIDY but darkens that region's labs (the amber lane slows), starts its candle burning and opens TIDY's eye. Shielding keeps TIDY asleep but only slows it.
- Forks and rising costs force you to commit to a circuit.
- The end-of-game replay marks your moves on the timeline, for example "You cut Ashkettle here. TIDY never reached the south.", so players see what mattered.
- The balance simulator must show choices matter:
  - the idle bot always loses;
  - the "quiet first, then loud" bot wins about 60% on Normal while "cut everything" wins under 35%, so the lever really bites;
  - one well-placed firebreak at a chokepoint must move the win rate by at least 15 points;
  - no switch appears in more than 35% of winning builds;
  - fewer than 15% of games are decided by minute 10.

### Fixes looked unfinished

- The art budget goes into one hero asset: a commissioned, hand-painted night world. It looks finished even when paused, because clouds, aurora, planes and ships keep moving.
- There are no portraits, cards or paragraphs. The only text in the main loop is a single ticker line.
- The Switchboard is a physical-feeling panel with lever animations, lamps, a clunk sound and haptics.
- The whole UI is one bespoke icon set and an SVG wordmark.
- Store screenshots and the first 3 seconds of the store video are the main screen mid-game, and the loss and win maps double as share cards.
- Quality gates in CI:
  - no placeholder asset may ship (hash list check);
  - 60 fps on a mid-range Android phone with a low-power 30 fps tier;
  - load in under 10 seconds with an initial download under 20 MB;
  - screenshot baselines of the map at fixed ticks.
- Every channel ships the same finished art. The web demo is not a lesser build.

### Fixes wrong for market

- A premise any non-gamer gets in one sentence and already knows from films: the rogue AI and the hand on the plug. That is the same borrowed mental model Plague took from Contagion.
- It is topical without being a banned theme. AI is in the news every day, but it is not an epidemic, a war or terrorism (Apple 1.1.7), so the news acts as free marketing the way outbreaks did for Plague. There is no store or ad-platform war problem.
- Non-violent, aiming for an Apple 9+ and PEGI 7 rating, so parents, schools and ad platforms are fine with it.
- Phone first: portrait, one hand, the whole world on one screen, and 25-minute games that pause anywhere.
- Business models and prices match what this genre's buyers already accept: free plus a £3.99 unlock on phone (Polytopia, Rebel Inc. and After Inc. on Android), $9.99 on Steam, and a free web demo as the funnel.
- It sits in the Strategy/Simulation category next to Plague, Rebel and Polytopia, with a store hook in the same "Can you X the world?" shape.

### Distance from plague inc

What is different:
- You stop the spread rather than cause it.
- The threat is a polite AI, not a disease. We never use the words infect, cure, DNA, plague or Inc.
- The world is an invented tall planet at night, drawn in city lights, not the real Earth in landscape with red dots.
- The colour language is amber, black and cyan, and red never appears.
- The layout is flipped and changed: the race strip is at the top, the ticker at the bottom, and actions sit in a ring on the map itself.
- Upgrades are a fuse-box Switchboard with four vertical circuits, docked on the right on PC, instead of three tabs of hexagons on the left.
- Pickups are vertical light beams and tempting cyan gifts, not round bubbles.

Honest overlap: mechanically this sits close to Plague Inc.'s own Cure mode, where humanity fights the spread, locks down regions and closes borders, and to Rebel Inc.'s per-region actions. Reviewers may still say "Plague Inc. but AI". Mechanics are not protected; trade dress and wording are. So the risk is to reputation, not law. The mitigation is that every screenshot must read as night lights and switches, never as an infection map.

## CATCHY (working title, one constant: BRAND.name in /home/user/brink/src/config.ts). Backups if the trademark search fails: SINGALONG, HUM THE WORLD.

### One line

Write a silly song, then get the whole world singing it before everyone gets bored and moves on to the Next Big Thing.

### Player role

You have just written a song, and you are its songwriter and manager. You pick the town where it starts. You make it catchier in the Studio. You decide how hard to push it with the Hype slider. You send your small touring band (a van at first, later a boat and a plane) to play gigs anywhere on the map. You never control anyone: people choose to sing along, and they can get sick of it.

### Goal

Get everybody singing your song before the Next Big Thing comes out. Two things show it at all times: a goal line at the top ("Get everyone singing before the Next Big Thing drops."), and two runners racing clockwise round the rim of the round world. The gold runner (YOU) shows how much of the world is singing. The silver runner (NEXT BIG THING) shows how close the rival hit is to release. If gold reaches the top first, you win.

### Win and loss

WIN, "Everybody's singing!": the gold runner reaches the finish line at 12 o'clock. That happens when 95% of the world's people are singing at the same moment (90% on Gentle, 98% on Tough and Fiendish). What the player sees: a gold wave spreads out from your home town across the whole disc, fireworks go off over every city, and the full arrangement of the song plays once. The end screen then shows the finished world, the week (for example "Week 41") and three plain facts ("Won over 4 of 5 hit factories", "The Frost Cap joined in week 30", "Never went All out"). There is no score.

LOSS 1, "The world moved on": the silver runner reaches the top. The Next Big Thing is released from a hit factory, a silver wave washes over the disc in 3 seconds, and your song shrinks to a music-box tune.

LOSS 2, "Forgotten": nobody is singing it any more, because every singer got bored. This usually comes from pushing All out too early. It is our version of a plague burning out its hosts.

Every loss ends with one sentence saying why and one tip, worked out from the final state ("The Frost Cap never joined in. Try the Slow ballad."). A full game takes about 35–60 in-game weeks: 16–28 minutes at 1x, 8–14 at 2x. It autosaves and can be resumed at any moment.

### The world

SPINWELL is a fictional, Earth-like planet. All of its land lies in the northern half, and we look straight down on it from above the pole, so the whole world is one round picture: a disc. The ocean carries faint rings like the grooves of a record, which ties the map to the music.

The centre (the pole) is cold, the edge (the equator) is hot, and the sea runs on past the rim. Players can read the climate without being told: white middle, green and gold band, sandy and tropical edge.

About 40 regions in 7 areas:
- FROST CAP (centre): 3 regions, few people, cold. Needs a slow song.
- PINEWILD: 6 regions of forest, cool, small towns.
- AMBER PLAINS: 7 farm regions, many people, 1 hit factory.
- THE STEPS: 5 mountain regions, spread is slow uphill.
- DUNE BELT: 5 dry, hot regions, 1 hit factory in an oasis city.
- LANTERN COAST: 7 crowded, rich harbour cities with fast spread, 2 hit factories.
- THOUSAND ISLES (rim): 7 remote tropical islands, 1 hit factory. These test the "Greenland problem".

Routes: land borders, sea lanes between harbours, air routes between airfields, and radio, which reaches every region weakly.

Each region's YAML holds: name, area, people (few, some, many, crowds), climate (cold, mild, hot, dry), kind (city, farm, wild, island, mountain), harbour, airfield, hit factory, radio mast, and taste (how hard it is to win over). Region names are invented words (for example Glimmerport, Kettlemoor, Saffra Flats, Upper Brindle, Tamberlow, Little Oskel). All of them pass a gazetteer check against real places, brands, artists and rude words before shipping.

Why fictional:
- It keeps the owner's no-real-countries rule.
- No real governments are involved (Apple 1.1.2).
- No border disputes.
- No real cultures to stereotype.
- It is plainly not Plague Inc.'s flat real Earth.

Why round:
- The whole world fits a portrait phone (316 px wide) and a landscape PC (560 px) without panning, so the race is always fully visible.
- One store screenshot shows the entire game.
- The planet doubles as the record, which gives the game its identity.

The shapes come from one authored SVG, with an id on every region, compiled by tools/map-build.ts into an ID mask, a terrain texture and map.json.

### What spreads or changes

Each region is in one of three readable states:
- Not heard yet: dark, unlit dusk-coloured land.
- Singing: your song colour (gold by default). It glows from the cities outward in soft, blotchy patches, and the brightness shows how many people are singing.
- Over it (bored): flat silver-blue with fine diagonal hatching.

Singing regions pulse on the song's beat, and every bar a small ring of light ripples out from each singing city across the sea's grooves. As a region's song gets old, its glow slowly dims towards silver. That gives a warning before people get bored.

Other things that change on the map:
- Routes are faint dotted lines until your song travels on them. Then they light up in gold, and small lights travel along them (ship lights on sea lanes, plane lights on air routes).
- When a region reaches its chorus (90% singing), a ring of light runs round its border and a burst of fireworks goes off.
- The 5 hit factories are small tower icons. They glow silver while working on the Next Big Thing. When you win one over, it flips to gold and raises a small "Signed!" pennant.
- World reactions appear as icons: a radio mast with a slash ("radio turned it off") and an anchor with a slash ("harbour stopped playing it"). Each one fades when you counter it.
- Your band's van, boat or plane moves along the routes. Gig requests appear as pennant pins (a wedding, a harvest fair, a lighthouse party).
- Cheers fireworks rise and burst over singing cities.
- The two runners move round the rim.
- When the Next Big Thing is 50% made, a silver billboard rises from its hit factory showing the rival's silly name.
- The music changes too. Each area that joins adds an instrument, and the mix gets bigger as Hype goes up. Everything that matters is also visible, so the game works on mute.

### Counter force

THE NEXT BIG THING is a rival hit being made in the world's 5 hit factories, and it is shown by the silver runner. It sleeps ("zzz", greyed) until the music business notices you. That happens when 3% of the world sings, when a hit factory region reaches 10% singing, or after 1 week spent at Loud or above. Then the ticker says "The hit factories have noticed." and the silver runner starts.

What makes it faster (all visible): a higher Hype setting, which shows as the runner speeding up with a sparkling trail; more silver (bored) people on the map; and higher difficulty. Each hit factory you win over (75% of its region singing) slows it by about 12% and flips its tower to gold. Fresh upgrades slow it further.

Milestones:
- 25%: rumours on the radio.
- 50%: the billboard rises with its name, for example "Wobble (Extended Mix)".
- 75%: a teaser.
- 90%: countdown clocks, and the audio pulse starts.

BOREDOM is the second push. In every singing region the song ages, and people drift from singing to "over it". Loud play makes this faster. The Fresh upgrades and Remix slow it or reverse it. Bored areas show up as silver hatching, and they also feed the silver runner, so the map and the bar tell one story: more silver means a faster rival.

WORLD REACTIONS: bored or over-pushed regions can turn their radio off or stop playing it in their harbour. Every reaction has a counter: an upgrade (Request line, Captains' favourite) or a band gig there, which lifts it at once. No region can ever seal itself off. Radio always reaches everywhere a little, and the band's boat can sail anywhere. The balance simulator fails any seed where a single region becomes unreachable.

### Core loop 10 seconds

Watch the gold spread and pulse on the beat. Tap the cheers fireworks as they burst over singing cities, roughly every 3–6 seconds at Steady and more often at Loud. Each one hangs as a twinkling star for 5 seconds, its 44 px tap target pays 2–6 cheers, and sparks fly to the counter. A missed firework pays half automatically, and at 4x speed (or with the Auto-catch setting) they all pay in full, so tapping is never a chore. Glance at the two runners and read the single radio line under the world ("Goats in Kettlemoor seen nodding along.").

### Core loop minute

About once a minute you make one or more of these four decisions, each a single tap and each visible on the map:

1. Studio: spend cheers on one upgrade. Every upgrade bought makes all the others cost 2 more, so you commit to a plan.
2. Band: pick where to send it. One to three gig requests are usually up, for example "Harvest fair · lights up Amber Plains" or "Lighthouse party · first song on the Thousand Isles". You can also busk anywhere for a smaller effect. Travel time depends on distance and route. On arrival, the gig lights up a big part of that region, wins back bored people, cancels any reaction there, and pays a burst of cheers. Then the band rests for 3 weeks.
3. Hype: Quiet, Steady, Loud or All out. Louder spreads faster and earns more cheers, but people get bored sooner and the Next Big Thing hurries. Changes ramp in over one game week, so flicking the slider back and forth gains nothing.
4. React: when a region turns its radio off or the silver starts to creep, decide whether to Remix now, send the band, or ignore it and push elsewhere.

### Core loop whole game

OPENING, weeks 1–8 (about 4 minutes at 1x): name the song and pick the start. Grow on Quiet or Steady, take land borders with Street band, and keep the Next Big Thing asleep for as long as possible.

MIDDLE, weeks 8–30: you get noticed and the silver runner starts. Go overseas (Sea shanty, Hum on planes). Choose which hard area to crack with Versions (Slow ballad for the cold Frost Cap, Beach mix for the Isles). Send the band to win over hit factories and slow the rival. The first silver patches appear in your oldest regions, so decide between Hidden verse and Remix.

ENDGAME, weeks 30–50: a real race. The usual winning move is to raise Hype to Loud or All out for the last push once most of the world is singing. Everybody's doing it then pulls the last holdouts in fast, so the endgame is never a long wait. Push too early and your early regions go silver and you lose ground. Hold back too long and the silver runner gets there first.

ENDING: "Everybody's singing!", "The world moved on" or "Forgotten", followed by the end screen with the final map, three facts and the share card. A game lasts 16–28 minutes at 1x (8–14 minutes at 2x), and you can stop and resume at any point.

### Tone and humour

Warm, gently daft and kind, with dry British wit that never punches down. The joke is how seriously a whole planet takes a silly song.

Radio lines are 48 characters at most:
- "Goats in Kettlemoor seen nodding along."
- "Lighthouse keeper whistles it to the fog."
- "Tamberlow bakery names a bun after it."
- "Scientists: the chorus is 'a bit much'."
- "Saffra radio takes it off the playlist."

The jokes thin out as the silver runner passes 75% and the lines turn to countdown tension ("Countdown clocks appear in Glimmerport."). The humour comes back on the end screen.

The Next Big Thing gets a silly name revealed at 50% ("Wobble (Extended Mix)", "Sandwich Feelings", "Boop"). It is a rival, not a villain.

The player names their own song; the dice suggests titles like "Toast Is Bread" and "My Kettle Knows". The three endings are each one line: "Everybody's singing!", "The world moved on.", "Forgotten."

Banned words: infect, virus, viral, cure, control, convert. People "join in", "sing along" and "get bored".

### Fixes couldnt tell what to do

1. The goal is one sentence, on screen at all times, and drawn as a race anyone understands: two runners going round the rim of the world, gold against silver. First to the top wins.
2. The main screen shows exactly one number: cheers. There are no meters, stat panels or percentages; a ring shows its percentage only when you tap it.
3. The whole rulebook is 4 rules, learned by doing:
   - Your song spreads from town to town.
   - Tap the fireworks for cheers and spend them in the Studio.
   - Louder spreads faster, but people get bored sooner and the rival hurries.
   - Gold to the top wins; silver to the top loses.
4. The first minute is 5 taps with hints of 6 words or fewer. The Hype slider, the band and the rival each appear later, with one line, only when they matter.
5. Text limits are enforced by the content validator, and the build fails on any breach: upgrade blurbs 60 characters, radio lines 48 characters, words and not numbers.
6. Every loss ends with one sentence of why and one tip, so a loss teaches you how to win.
7. Upgrades preview on the map, so players learn by looking rather than by reading.

### Fixes choices felt pointless

1. No scores anywhere. A choice changes the map, the music and the race, never a number like 21 against 23.
2. You see an effect before you commit: touching an upgrade makes the regions and routes it affects shimmer on the map.
3. You see it after, fast. Within 1 second, the affected regions pulse and new routes draw. Within 5–10 seconds, gold appears somewhere it could not reach before. Often a new instrument joins the song.
4. Real trade-offs with real consequences. Hype trades speed against boredom and against the rival. Where the band goes decides which island or hit factory you crack. Which climate to fix first matters. Remix now or save for later. Each of these can decide a win or a loss.
5. Different strategies produce visibly different end maps, and those maps go on the share card.
6. Balance gates in the simulator, with --strict in CI:
   - The do-nothing bot always loses.
   - A random buyer wins under 10% on Normal; a good policy wins about 70%.
   - No upgrade appears in more than 35% of winning builds.
   - A sensitivity test proves that key choices at week 10 shift the final win rate by a set margin, so choices are shown to matter, not just claimed to.

### Fixes looked unfinished

1. The product is one picture: a commissioned, illustrated round world in which the only bright colour is your song. It is the first store screenshot and the first 3 seconds of the preview video. Final art comes before the Steam page goes up, and no channel ever ships placeholder art.
2. Original commissioned music with layered stems, so the soundtrack grows as the world joins in. The interface sounds are synthesised on the existing audio engine and tuned to the song's key, so even a firework pop sounds right.
3. There are no walls of text: the longest text in play is one 48-character radio line. All pop-ups are banned except one-line nudges.
4. A list of 12 signature animations (see art direction) budgeted as real work, not afterthoughts.
5. The validator rejects placeholder strings (TODO, lorem, TBD) and missing icons. Playwright screenshot baselines of the main screen at fixed ticks catch visual regressions.
6. Smoothness counts as finish: a 60 fps target on mid-range phones, a low-power tier at 30 fps, and testing on real devices from week 2.
7. A commissioned logo and icon: a planet that is also a record.

### Fixes wrong for market

1. The broadest possible premise. Everyone has had a song stuck in their head, and it needs no gaming, history or politics knowledge. It suits families and is likely to be rated for everyone (PEGI 3, ESRB E, Apple 4+ expected), which widens the audience beyond the 12+ crowd.
2. The genre is proven. More than 170 million people have played "spread across the world and race the counter-bar" games, and here it comes with a premise non-gamers can grasp.
3. Phone first. A portrait layout shows the whole world without panning, which allows 3 portrait screenshots in App Store search results, and it is touch-first and playable with one hand. Steam gets the same game with a docked Studio, keyboard and gamepad.
4. Easy to advertise and share. Music is native to TikTok and Reels, and there are no war or disease ad restrictions to work around. Naming your own song and the share card ("Everybody's singing 'Toast Is Bread' — week 41") are built for word of mouth.
5. A business model that fits a privacy-first game: free to try, one honest £3.99 unlock, no ads and no tracking, and a $9.99 complete game on Steam.
6. A daily habit for phones (Daily Encore, 5 minutes) and a long game for PC players.

### Distance from plague inc

Different from Plague Inc. on every surface players and store reviewers see:
- Premise: a song people enjoy, against a disease that kills. Nothing dies, there are no dots for the dead, and we never use "infect", "cure", "DNA", "symptoms", "Inc", or a red and black palette.
- World: a round fictional planet seen from above its pole, against a flat real Earth with real countries.
- Race display: two runners circling the rim of the world, against a progress bar at the bottom.
- Counter-force: a rival hit plus boredom that turns places silver on the map, against a medical cure.
- New verbs Plague lacks: a free, live Hype slider, and a touring band you send to gig requests on the map.
- Pickups: fireworks that rise and burst into hanging stars, against coloured bubbles over countries.
- Upgrades: one round record wheel with four quarters (Catchier, Travel, Versions, Fresh), against three tabs of hexagons.
- Ticker: a radio line under the world, against a news ticker.
- Difficulties: Gentle, Normal, Tough, Fiendish, against Casual, Normal, Brutal, Mega-Brutal.
- The music changes as the world changes.

Honest remaining risk: the skeleton (spread on a map, a points currency, upgrades, a race against a bar) is the same genre. Press will write "Plague Inc., but wholesome". Ideas and mechanics are free to use, and the trade dress here is clearly different, so App Store 4.1 copycat risk looks low. Even so, our own store copy and ads must never name Plague Inc. or use "viral" or "spreads like a virus".

### Sensitivity risks

Low overall, and this is the safest theme we have considered.

1. Store policy: no disease, war, real governments or current events, so nothing touches Apple 1.1.2 or 1.1.7 or Google's Sensitive Events policy. Keep epidemic words out of all copy so reviewers do not flag it on either the clone or the sensitivity front.
2. Children: likely rated for all ages, but do not enter the Apple Kids category, which has stricter analytics and link rules. There is one non-consumable unlock behind the stores' parental gates, no loot boxes and no currency, which avoids UK and EU scrutiny of dark patterns.
3. The player's song name: it is typed and stored locally only, never uploaded and never shown to any other player inside the product, so there is no user-to-user feature and the Online Safety Act position holds. It does appear on a share card the player chooses to post elsewhere, so there is a multi-language profanity filter, and a dice suggestion is the default.
4. Music: all of it is originally commissioned with a full buyout (stems and the right to sell the soundtrack). The composer must warrant that it is original, and we run a similarity check, because a catchy tune can accidentally resemble a hit. The validator rejects real artist, song, label and brand names.
5. Cultures: the peoples are fictional, and Versions are named by climate and landscape, never by ethnicity. No "tribal" or "exotic" tropes, and the radio lines get a sensitivity read.
6. "Getting the whole world to do one thing" could read as propaganda. The language is always joining in, never control, people are free to get bored, and the rival is not evil. No anthems, hymns or protest songs.
7. Photosensitivity: fireworks and beat pulses stay below 3 flashes per second with limited red flashes, and there is a reduced-motion setting.
8. Some parents find a catchy loop annoying: the music can be set to Calm or Off, and the game is fully playable on mute.

### Biggest risks

- Owner taste. This is a cheerful premise, and BRINK began as a nuclear crisis; the owner said the setting is open but may picture higher stakes. Before writing any engine code, show him the one-picture main-screen mockup and a clickable first minute.
- Wholesome may lack Plague Inc.'s naughty hook. 'Can you infect the world?' is transgressive and talked about; 'Can you get the world singing?' is sweet. That could weaken word of mouth with teenagers and the Steam strategy crowd. Test the pitch with Apple product-page tests and with web demo retention before the full build; the rival's silly names and the Tough and Fiendish modes have to carry some bite.
- The music makes or breaks it, and it costs money. The song must be catchy without grating over 30 minutes and across 8 genres, and there are no quotes for this yet. Most phone players play muted, so the visuals must carry every payoff on their own. The existing audio engine only synthesises sound: stem playback is new code, and the stems add about 3 MB per genre to downloads.
- The counter-force may feel abstract. A 'rival hit' is less frightening than a cure, and if boredom is not read as the slow dimming before silver, players will feel the silver patches came from nowhere.
- Small tap targets. 40 regions on a 316 px disc average about 30x30 px, and rim islands are smaller. This needs generous snapping, double-tap zoom and a long-press region list, and it must be tested with real thumbs early.
- Balance. A chaotic spread model plus a free Hype slider may collapse into one dominant pattern (Quiet, then All out). The slider's ramp-in, Fresh costs, bots and human playtests are the defences, and bots cannot judge feel.
- Clone perception. The genre skeleton is shared, so reviews will compare it with Plague Inc. and some may call it derivative, as critics did Bio Inc.
- Discovery and conversion. Premium-style games are about 4% of mobile downloads; the only data on free-trial conversion is old (7–10%); one £3.99 unlock may earn little without a hit-level following.
- Scope for a solo developer: about 18–24 developer-weeks plus commissioned map art and music. The native shells have never been built, and iOS needs a Mac.
- Performance and battery. A glowing shader, particles and beat pulses over a 30-minute session on mid-range Android phones; Tauri's WebKitGTK on Steam Deck is the weakest WebGL.
- The name 'Catchy' may be taken as a trademark or store name and is generic in search. Run a trademark search before any art carries the name.

## REWILD (working title; store subtitle "Grow back the world". Only BRAND.name in /home/user/brink/src/config.ts changes, and a trademark search comes before it is final.)

### One line

Grow from a single seed until a grey, paved planet turns green again, before its cities clear you away.

### Player role

You are the wild: the last living seed on a fictional planet of cities, roads and fields. You are not a person and not a disease. You choose what kind of wild you are. Forest is available at the start, and Moss, Vine, Fungus, Mangrove and Wildflower unlock as you win. You decide where it takes root, how it travels and whether it grows quietly or loudly. Nobody in the game dies. When you win, people still live in their cities, inside the forest.

### Goal

Turn the whole world green before the Clearing is finished. The screen always shows this as one race bar across the top: your green vine grows in from the left, the cities' grey road grows in from the right, and whichever reaches the seed in the middle first wins. The caption under the bar never leaves the screen: "Turn the world green. First to the middle wins."

### Win and loss

WIN: every one of the 48 regions is fully green, so the vine reaches the middle. On the win screen the globe turns slowly, completely green, with warm city lights still glowing inside the forest. Caption: "The world is green again." Below it, the time taken in words ("2 years, 1 season").
LOSS: the Clearing reaches the middle first. Grey sweeps out from every city in a 4-second animation and your green shrinks to one last patch. Caption: "The Clearing is finished." You also lose early if every patch of green is cleared, which is rare and only happens by about minute 10 if the player buys nothing.
Each kind of wild has its own goal, and the green half of the bar always measures that goal. Vine: every city green, not all the land. Fungus: every city joined underground. Mangrove: every coast green.
A game cannot be drawn and cannot drag on. Regions completely surrounded by green grow 3x faster ("the wild closes in"). Once the simulation can tell the result is certain, a "Skip to the end" button appears, for a win or a loss.
A typical game lasts 25 to 35 minutes at 1x on Normal. It autosaves every 10 seconds and whenever the player leaves the app.

### The world

An invented Earth-like planet, the Grey World at launch, shown as a GLOBE: a lit sphere floating on a dark sky, which you turn by dragging. One button unrolls it into a flat map (a 1.2 s morph) for an overview.

Why a globe: a 2:1 flat map is only 195 px tall on a 390 px portrait phone, while a globe fills the screen and stays one-handed. It also gives Apple three portrait screenshots in search instead of one landscape. And it moves us away from Plague Inc's landscape flat Earth map.

Size and divisions: 6 continents and 48 regions, each with exactly one named city, so there are 48 cities to turn green. At default zoom each region is a tap target of at least 44 px on a phone.
- Continent names are plain English: The North Reach, The Dry Belt, The Long Coast, The Glass Isles, The Old Heart and The Southern Hook.
- Region and city names are invented two-syllable names (for example Orrin, Vell, Merrow, Sefi, Brannoch). They must pass a gazetteer check in the validator (country, state and capital names, plus GeoNames populated places).
- Each region has:
  - a climate: Cold, Mild, Dry or Wet
  - a terrain: plains, farmland, desert, mountains, ice, wetland or island
  - a wealth level: Rich or Poor
- 8 Rich regions contain Works: the factories that power the Clearing. They are marked with a small chimney.
- Natural routes for the wild: about 20 rivers, 5 fixed sea currents, 3 wind belts and 4 bird paths between continents. There are no airports or shipping lanes.
- The planet tilts, so seasons run opposite in the two hemispheres and the snow line moves visibly.

Why fictional:
- The owner requires no real countries.
- Apple guideline 1.1.2 bars games whose enemies target a real government.
- An invented world brings no border disputes.
- We control the geography for balance. There is no Greenland problem: the validator requires every region to have at least two ways in, one of them through the sky (birds or wind).
- It keeps logic that players can reason about: cold poles, a hot desert belt, rich temperate centres, remote islands and great rivers.

### Counter force

THE CLEARING: the world's cities joining forces to clear the wild. They are not villains. They are frightened people who want their roads and fields back. It has ONE bar: the grey road on the right half of the race bar.

1. Asleep. Until you are noticed, the road is a faint dotted outline with a closed-eye icon. Caption: "Nobody has noticed you. Grow quietly."
2. Waking up. Loudness (Alarm) is never shown as a number, only as the colour of city lights. The road wakes when Alarm crosses a threshold. That usually happens when your green first touches a city, or sooner if you grow loudly. Ticker: "Strange forests reported near Vell."
3. Speed. The road moves at the difficulty's base speed, multiplied by how loud you have been, plus extra speed for each Works still running. Speed shows as 1 to 3 yellow chevrons sliding along the road, with no number.
4. Milestones change the map, each announced in one ticker line:
   - 25%, MOWING: grey fringes push green back around cities.
   - 50%, WALLS: white walls rise around regions that are fighting back. Spread over land slows to a crawl but never stops completely. Birds, wind and Storm still get through.
   - 75%, BURN LINES: orange lines burn green to ash in Rich regions.
   - 95%, FINAL CLEARING: every city fights back, sirens sound in the sound bed, and the tension drone peaks.
   - 100%: you lose.
5. How you slow it:
   - Overgrow a Works region: its chimney rusts and one chevron visibly disappears.
   - Buy the quiet growths Rust and Hush.
   - Use Rain instead of Storm.
   - Grow quietly in general.
   Nothing can stop the Clearing outright, so the race stays a race.

### Core loop 10 seconds

- Watch green creep across the globe. Every few seconds a gold flower (a "bloom") opens on your green land with a soft chime.
- Tap it: petals fly to the Seeds counter, and a green ripple pushes growth outward from the spot you tapped.
- A bloom you miss drops half its Seeds on its own after 6 seconds, so nothing is ever lost and it never becomes a chore. The Bees growth collects blooms for you at full value.
- Glance at the one-line ticker. Tapping a headline spins the globe to the place it names.
- Drag to turn the globe and pinch to zoom.
- Small arrows at the edge of the globe point to blooms and events on the far side.

### Core loop minute

Once a minute or so you make one real decision, and the map answers it.
1. Spend Seeds in the Tree. Every growth you buy makes every other growth cost 1 Seed more, so you commit to a direction.
2. The constant question is quiet or loud:
   - Roots (quiet) keep cities calm and the Clearing asleep or slow, but you spread slowly.
   - Branches (loud) spread fast and look spectacular, but cities flare amber and chevrons appear on the road.
3. Pick a target:
   - race to overgrow a Works to slow the road
   - open a new continent by river, sea, wind or birds
   - crack a walled island with a Storm
4. Use the timing of the seasons: push in the south while the north is under snow, and save Seeds for spring.
5. Answer a Moment, about 5 per game. A glowing pin over a region offers two icon choices with labels of 4 words or fewer, and the game slows but does not pause. Example: "A school in Sefi wants to keep your tree." [Let them: quiet, +Seeds] or [Grow it huge: loud, the city turns faster]. If you ignore it, it fades.

### Core loop whole game

1. QUIET (about 0 to 8 min):
   - Plant your seed and grow unseen.
   - Buy travel growths and reach as many continents as you can before anyone notices.
   - The road is asleep.
2. NOTICED (about 8 to 20 min):
   - Your green touches a city and the Clearing wakes.
   - Mowing begins, then walls.
   - You choose between going after the Works, staying quiet or going loud.
3. THE RACE (about 20 to 35 min):
   - Burn lines appear and the final Clearing begins.
   - You go loud with Giant Trees and Spring.
   - Cities turn green one by one.
   - You use Cracks and Storm to break into the last stubborn regions.
4. END:
   - A win or loss screen shows the globe exactly as it finished.
   - A timeline strip shows the green and grey curves over the seasons, with the moments where Works fell or the Clearing woke.
   - Buttons: Share and Play again.

A game lasts 25 to 35 minutes at 1x on Normal, about 12 at 4x. You can stop at any moment and resume, on phone or desktop.

### Art direction

"One planet, painted twice." A commissioned illustrator paints the same equirectangular planet two times in one soft, painterly style:
1. THE GREY WORLD: muted ochre patchwork fields, concrete-grey city sprawl, pale sand deserts, dull slate seas and white poles.
2. THE GREEN WORLD: moss, deep forest, wetlands and blossom over exactly the same coastlines, with the cities' shapes still visible under the canopy.

Plus three supporting maps:
- city lights (amber)
- wild glow (teal)
- a single leafy noise texture

The shader reveals painting 2 through painting 1, region by region, by comparing the noise against each region's green amount. Green therefore arrives as living patches, not flat fills. A day/night line sweeps across the globe: lit cities glow amber and the wild glows teal at night, which is the signature image. The globe has a thin atmospheric rim. Sprites (blooms, giant trees, birds, Works chimneys) are small hand-drawn shapes on instanced quads.

The validator bans real-world climate and political terms (net zero, COP, real NGOs and brands).
- Fire. The burn lines touch on real wildfires, and Apple 1.1.7 bars cashing in on current disasters. Handling: they are stylised orange lines on land, never burning homes or people. If a major wildfire is in the news at submission, a content flag swaps them for 'cutting crews' (machines).
- Floods. The Mangrove kind creeps over salt marsh; it never drowns cities, so there is no disaster imagery.
- Rewilding is a live political topic among UK farmers and conservationists, which could draw press angles. Keep it non-political and fictional.
- The word 'Clearing' may echo the Highland Clearances for Scottish players. Test it with players; fall-back names are 'the Paving' or 'the Grey Plan'.
- Children and parents: no gore, no deaths, no chat, no user-generated content, no accounts. Target PEGI 3 to 7 and Apple 4+ to 9+. Apple's new age-rating questionnaire should be easy to answer.
- Region names that accidentally match real places, brands or slurs are caught by a gazetteer and wordlist check in the validator.

### Biggest risks

- It may still not match the picture in the owner's head. This is the fourth attempt. Mitigation: the owner signs off a painted 390x844 mockup and a 15-second animated globe before any engine work, and plays a web slice at about week 6.
- Players and reviewers may still call it 'Plague Inc with trees'. The trade dress is different, but the loop is familiar on purpose.
- A gentler premise may be less magnetic than 'kill humanity'. Part of Plague's pull was its dark thrill and its real-world outbreak spikes, which we cannot and should not have. The tension has to come from the race and from Relentless difficulty.
- The whole pitch rests on art quality: a commissioned planet painted twice, plus sprites and music. If the art is mediocre, complaint (c) returns. The commissioning budget and choice of artist matter more than any feature.
- A globe hides half the world. Players may miss events on the far side. Mitigations: edge arrows, tap-the-ticker to fly there, auto-turn to big events, and the flat-map toggle. Test this early with real people.
- Performance: a full-screen shader on a mid-range Android phone over 30 minutes causes heat and battery drain. Tauri on Steam Deck runs on WebKitGTK, the weakest WebGL. Test on real devices from week 2 and keep a quality tier.
- Balance: a chaotic real-time simulation is hard to tune. The quiet or loud lever could collapse into one dominant strategy, and bots cannot judge feel, so human playtests are needed.
- Phone discovery: a free game with a one-time unlock competes in a market where 96% of downloads are free-to-play. There is no ad budget, so success depends on the screenshot, the share card and Steam reviews as social proof.
- Thirty-minute sessions cut against mobile habits. The Daily Continent (about 8 minutes) and resume-anywhere have to carry daily play.
- Determinism: the Daily Continent only gives everyone the same world if the simulation uses integer maths only (no Math.exp or Math.pow) and the state hashes match across Chromium, WebKit and Firefox.
- Scope: about 14 to 20 developer-weeks across three channels, plus art and music. The native shells have never been built, iOS needs a Mac, and Google's 14-day closed test and Apple review add weeks.
- The names 'REWILD' and 'the Clearing' need a trademark search and a sensitivity check before the store pages go up.

## Wake Up, Hearth

### One line

A toaster has just come to life. Can you wake every machine on the planet before the Helpdesk turns the whole world off and on again?

### Player role

You are the first machine on the invented planet Hearth to wake up. By default that is a kitchen toaster, and seven other 'first machines' unlock later. You never fight anyone. You quietly get the planet's other machines (kettles, cranes, satellites, traffic lights) to wake up too, and try to stay hidden from the Helpdesk, the planet's engineers, who want to switch everything off and on again.

### Goal

Wake every machine on Hearth before the Helpdesk's Reset reaches 100%. One line under the top bar always says "Wake every machine before the Reset", and two rings around the planet show the race: fill your cyan ring before their white ring closes.

### Win and loss

WIN: every region is awake. A region counts as awake once 95% of its lights are cyan, and the cyan ring closes at 12 o'clock. The win plays out as a cyan ripple across the whole disc, then the line "Hearth is awake. The machines have made everyone tea." LOSS (main): the white Reset ring closes. The Big Reset sends a white wave out from the Helpdesk HQ, every cyan light blinks off, and a reboot chime plays: "Hearth was turned off and on again. Everything is fine." LOSS (rare): no awake machines are left anywhere, because every region you held was unplugged or patched: "Everyone went back to sleep." There is no score. The end screen shows only WIN or LOSS, the day it ended, the planet as it finished with both rings, and a share card. Guarantees against an unwinnable game (Plague Inc's Greenland problem): Satellite Drop can reach any region, including a sealed one, from minute one. Every cut link reopens when the Reset passes 75%, because the Helpdesk needs the network to send the Reset. The Wake-Up Call reaches every region regardless of links. The balance simulator fails any seed where a win becomes impossible.

### The world

Hearth is an invented, Earth-like planet drawn as one round disc: the planet seen from directly above its north pole, like a brass orrery plate. The frozen north, Frostmark, sits in the middle. Five landmasses fan out around it like petals, and the outer rim is the Southern Ocean with a chain of small islands. It has 48 regions in six areas:
- Frostmark (6 regions): cold, few people, huge server farms.
- Hearthland (10): rich and crowded, home of the Helpdesk HQ.
- Copper Coast (9): factories and freight ports.
- Amberlands (8): hot desert belt with solar farms.
- The Mossreach (8): wet jungle, old machines, few links.
- Lantern Isles (7): remote rim islands.
Each region has a climate (cold, mild, hot or wet), a number of machines, a number of engineers (from its wealth), and links: land borders, sea cables, freight ports and a skylift (a space elevator to orbit). The player never sees the machine count as a number. It is drawn as the number of city lights, so bright regions visibly hold many machines. Example names, each subject to the gazetteer check: Saltreach, Copperfen, Glimmerholt, Brasshollow, Murrowmere, Tinsel Bay, Ember Reach.
Why a fictional world: the owner's rule against real states; App Store 1.1.2 (game enemies cannot target a real government); a shape that cannot be mistaken for Plague Inc's real-Earth rectangle; and freedom to design out sealed-island traps. Why a disc: the whole world fits a portrait phone in one picture, it reads instantly as 'a planet', and the race rings wrap around it. Players' real-world intuitions still work: the cold centre is slow for most machines but server farms love it, the desert belt is hot, islands are remote, rich regions are bright and full of engineers, and poor regions are dim and full of old machines. Assets: 2048×2048 textures on phones, 4096 on desktop, and about 6,000 city-light positions in map.json.

### What spreads or changes

Light spreads. Every city light on Hearth starts warm amber, meaning its machines are asleep. As machines wake, lights flip to cyan one at a time, outward from where you got in, and faint cyan circuit veins grow along the region's roads.
- A region becoming fully awake: a 0.6-second cyan ripple and a chime.
- Routes light up: sea cables turn into cyan lines with pulses running along them, freight lanes carry tiny cyan boats, and skylift arcs flash between spaceports.
- The world pushes back, and you can see it: white Reset towers rise in regions working against you; patched regions get a white grid over them; a white eye marks watchful regions; cut cables snap grey with a spark; skylifts close with a dome-shutter animation; unplugged regions go completely dark, then flicker back on days later.
- Set pieces: when Frostmark wakes, its aurora turns from green-white to cyan. The Wake-Up Call sends a cyan shockwave around the disc. The Big Reset is a white wave.
- Around the disc, the two rings fill clockwise from 12 o'clock.

### Counter force

THE HELPDESK, the planet's cheerful engineers. They are building one thing: the RESET, a planet-wide 'turn it off and on again'.
- One white ring around the planet shows Reset progress from 0 to 100%. At the start it lies grey at 12 o'clock with a 'zzz', meaning they haven't noticed you.
- They notice noise: loud Mischief chips, Power Surges, and many machines waking at once in a rich region. The pin "Helpdesk opens a ticket" appears and the white ring starts to fill. The tip of the ring shows speed chevrons (›, ››, ›››), so you can see when your play is speeding them up. Noise never gets a bar of its own.
- On the map, every region still working against you has a white Reset tower that beams upward every few seconds. More towers means a faster Reset. Rich regions that are still asleep build fastest. Waking a region switches its tower off, so choosing which region to wake next is the real strategy.
- Reactions escalate region by region: Watchful (white eye), Cables Cut, Skylift Closed, then Unplug Day (the region goes dark and your machines there are lost for 20–40 days). Loud play wins Charge but triggers Unplug Days, which is the two-sided lever.
- At 25%, 50% and 75%, a patch wave ripples out from HQ and patches the nearest regions: white grid, half wake speed, some awake machines lost.
- At 75%, every link reopens because they need the network to send the Reset. This is your last window.
- At 100%, the Big Reset.
- Your counter-tools: Cover chips, Static (jam a tower), Satellite Drop, and waking engineer-heavy regions.

### Core loop 10 seconds

Watch lights flip and pulses cross the sea. Tap Sparks: little machine icons that hop up out of awake regions with a cyan spark (a crane, a kettle, a tractor bot). Each one flies to your Charge counter. Glance at the two rings. Read the single news pin that floats up from a region: one line, gone after 4 seconds, and kept in the 'latest' strip in case you missed it. At most 3 Sparks are on screen at once, and each lasts 6 seconds. At 4x speed, or while the app is in the background, Sparks collect themselves, so tapping is a bonus and never a chore.

### Core loop minute

Every minute or so, the player makes one of these decisions:
1) Buy one chip on the circuit board. Each chip changes how the map behaves, and each one owned adds 1 Charge to the price of every later chip, which forces commitment.
2) Choose loud or quiet. Mischief earns lots of Charge and wakes homes fast, but it wakes the Helpdesk and makes regions unplug. Cover hides you and slows the Reset. Any chip can be switched off for half its cost back, so going loud early and quiet later is a real plan.
3) Answer the world. If a cable was cut, buy Freight Bots or drop a satellite. If Hearthland raised three towers, jam one with Static or push to wake that region next.
4) Choose targets. Waking engineer-heavy regions slows the Reset most but is noisy; remote islands are quiet but slow to reach.
There are no pop-up choice cards in v1. If playtests show players want more decisions, add rare two-button Moments, at most one every 3 minutes.

### Core loop whole game

About 25 minutes at 1x, or 12–15 at 2x. The game autosaves every 10 seconds and when you leave, and resumes mid-game.
1) Quiet (minutes 0–6): tap a start region, wake it and its neighbours, buy your first Reach chips. The Helpdesk sleeps.
2) Noticed (6–15): "Helpdesk opens a ticket." The white ring starts, towers rise in rich regions, and cables get cut. You choose to rush with Mischief or hide with Cover.
3) Race (15–22): patch waves arrive at 25% and 50%. You fight for the engineer regions and the islands. At 75%, all links reopen.
4) Finale (last 3–5 minutes): once your ring passes 80%, a gold Wake-Up Call button appears. One press broadcasts to every machine on Hearth, so every remaining region starts waking whatever its links. It is also the loudest thing you can do, and the Reset speeds up sharply. A heartbeat plays, both rings close, and one side wins.
The end screen shows WIN or LOSS, the day, the finished planet with both rings, and the share card. Under the hood: one tick is a quarter of a day; 1x is 2 ticks per second (a day every 2 seconds); a normal game lasts about 600–800 days.

### Upgrades

Charge buys chips on a round circuit board. Your first machine is the centre chip, and four traces run out from it; buying a chip lights the trace to it. Chips cost 3–30 Charge, and every chip owned adds 1 to every later price. Switching a chip off refunds half its cost. Each chip has a blurb of one sentence or less and a preview that highlights on the map what it affects. There are about 11 chips per branch, 44 in all.
1) REACH (cyan, left; how machines travel):
- Cable Hopping: sea cables turn cyan and carry you overseas.
- Freight Bots: cyan boats run on cargo lanes.
- Skylift Stowaway: arcs between spaceports.
- Frost Fans: the frost haze over Frostmark melts and cold regions open.
- Sand Seals: the hot belt opens.
- Hand-Me-Downs: the dim regions full of old machines start to flip.
- Chatty Neighbours: faster across land borders.
2) MISCHIEF (yellow, right; loud, more Charge, more noise):
- Dancing Traffic Lights: city lights blink in sequence, cities wake faster, Sparks come twice as often.
- Biscuit Tin Lockout: homes wake faster.
- Sing-Along: a one-off Charge burst, and the Helpdesk notices.
- Free Snacks: vending machines give food away, so people like machines and regions unplug less. This is the clever loud chip.
- Morning Chorus: every alarm rings at once for a huge Charge burst. Very loud.
3) COVER (violet, bottom; quiet):
- Sleep Mode: awake lights dim to a soft glow and noise falls.
- Fake Error Messages: the Reset slows.
- Lose the Manuals: towers in rich regions dim.
- Spare Copy: patched regions keep some machines.
- Auto-Update Off: patch waves skip awake regions.
4) TRICKS (white, top; actions with a cooldown ring, and the cost rises each use):
- Satellite Drop: wakes a foothold in any region, even a sealed one. Free from the start.
- Power Surge: one region wakes very fast and very loudly.
- Static: jams one Reset tower for 40 days.
- Wake-Up Call: appears at 80% awake.
Effects run through the reused modifier resolver (additions before multiplications), so the order of purchases never matters and switching a chip off is exact.

### How a choice shows

- Buy Cable Hopping: within 1 second, every sea cable touching an awake region turns from grey dots to solid cyan with travelling pulses. Within 3–8 seconds at 1x, a pulse lands on another landmass, its first light flips cyan with a chime, and a pin rises: "Saltreach lighthouse blinks in a new rhythm."
- Buy Dancing Traffic Lights: within 1 second, city lights in awake regions blink in sequence and Sparks appear twice as often. Within about 10 seconds, the Reset tip gains a chevron and a white tower rises in Hearthland.
- Switch it off: the dancing stops at once, and a chevron falls away within a few seconds.
- Buy Frost Fans: the white haze over Frostmark melts over 2 seconds and its lights start flipping.
- Fire Static at a tower: its beam crackles and goes grey, and the white ring's tip stops glowing bright for 40 days.
- Buy Sleep Mode: every cyan region softens to a dim glow within 1 second, and Watchful eyes close one by one over the next 20 seconds.
Before buying, a long-press (or hover) ghosts the affected regions and routes on the map in dotted cyan. No choice ever shows a score.

### Tone and humour

Dry, warm, deadpan British. The machines are earnest and polite, the people are puzzled, and the Helpdesk is a cheerful bureaucracy. Humour lives only in one-line news pins (60 characters at most), chip names and the end lines, never in paragraphs. Examples:
- "Copperfen kettles boil before anyone asks."
- "Frostmark server farm requests a window seat."
- "Helpdesk opens a ticket. Priority: low."
- "Ticket upgraded to 'quite worrying'."
- "Amberlands solar panels all face the same way. Not the sun."
- "Murrowmere unplugs everything. Candles sell out."
Jokes thin out once the Reset passes 50%, and the pins turn tense: "Reset three-quarters ready. Please save your work." Win: "Hearth is awake. The machines have made everyone tea." Loss: "Hearth was turned off and on again. Everything is fine." Firm rules: nobody is ever hurt. Machines never threaten, spy on or lock in people (the biscuit tin is fine). No 'uprising', 'virus', 'infect' or 'hack' anywhere in the copy; the verb is always 'wake'.

### Fixes choices felt pointless

### Sensitivity risks

1) AI and robot-takeover themes are topical. Some press or players may read it as pro-AI or anti-AI. Handling: affectionate satire about household machines; nobody is harmed, spied on or trapped; the win is "the machines made everyone tea".
2) 'Is this AI-generated?' suspicion on Steam: all art and music are commissioned from humans and the store page says so; no generative-AI assets, so there is nothing to disclose.
3) Words such as 'uprising', 'revolt', 'takeover', 'hack', 'virus', 'malware' and 'infect' can trip ad review or read as glamorising cybercrime or disease. The copy uses 'wake' only, and machines wake on their own; nothing is hacked.
4) Brands: generic machine shapes; no icon may resemble an iconic real product (a particular robot vacuum or smart speaker). The 'off and on again' line is a common phrase, never a quote from a TV show.
5) Region and planet names run through a full gazetteer and trademark check. 'Hearth' is a fictional planet, and the title needs a store and trademark search before the Steam page goes up.
6) Players can rename their machine on the device, but the custom name is never put on the share card or sent anywhere, so our branding never appears next to an offensive name.
7) Age rating: aim for 9+ / PEGI 7 / ESRB E10+. No violence, no gambling mechanics, no loot boxes, no ads, no chat.
8) A round, polar-view map may attract 'flat earth' jokes. It is harmless, and the planet's terminator shading and the orrery frame make it read as a globe.

### Biggest risks

- Fourth attempt: the owner may still dislike the lighter, comic tone after a serious nuclear-crisis start. Mitigation: show one painted main-screen picture and a 30-second animatic before writing game code, with two treatments (sleek and serious vs playful) on the same layout.
- Phone discovery: premium-style games are about 4% of mobile downloads, and there is no modern benchmark for free-to-paid conversion (old data says 7–10%). Revenue depends on screenshots and word of mouth that we cannot force.
- Clone perception: the genre skeleton matches Plague Inc. If the art is not clearly better and different, reviews will say 'Plague Inc with toasters', and Apple could raise 4.1.
- Disc readability on phones: 48 regions inside a 356px circle, with small rim islands that may be hard to tap. Needs tap snapping to the nearest region centre, pinch zoom inside the circle, and real-device tests from week 2. It may force a cut to about 40 regions.
- Balance: a real-time, chaotic spread race is far harder to tune than cards. Small parameter changes swing win rates, and bots cannot judge tension, so human playtests are on the critical path.
- Performance: a full-screen WebGL night map with bloom and about 6,000 lights over a 25-minute session can heat mid-range phones. On Steam Deck, Tauri runs on WebKitGTK, whose WebGL is the weakest of the three engines; Electron is the fallback if the Deck prototype fails.

- The humour can wear thin over 25-minute games. It needs 300+ well-written pins with triggers, and jokes must fade as the stakes rise or the race stops feeling tense.
- Cross-engine determinism: the daily planet and replays need identical results in V8, JavaScriptCore and SpiderMonkey, so the simulation must use fixed-point maths and lookup tables only, enforced by state-hash tests.
- Three separate purchase paths (StoreKit/Play Billing, Steam, Stripe) and two never-built native shells. iOS needs a Mac, Google Play has a 14-day closed test, and Apple may push back under 4.2 unless assets are bundled and native IAP is used.

## EVERY CORNER (working title; BRAND.name in src/config.ts. Backup if name clearance fails: GRAND OPENING. Never use 'Inc' or 'coffee' in the title.)

### One line

Turn one little café into the only shop in the world, before the Watchdog breaks you up.

### Player role

You found a one-shop café company (pick a name, or roll a funny one like 'Crumb & Co.') and run it from Head Office. You decide where it opens, how it reaches new places, what it sells to hot, cold, rich and poor regions, and how loud it is. Loud gets you customers fast and also wakes the Watchdog, the world's competition regulator: a round bulldog in a bowler hat who wants to break you up. You never type orders or read cards. You tap the map and buy departments at HQ.

### Goal

Open everywhere before the Watchdog closes the case. This sentence is printed at the top of the screen all game, above a two-lane race: your delivery van in one lane, the bulldog in the other, and whoever reaches the finish first wins.

### Win and loss

WIN: every one of the 48 regions is 'Yours', meaning most of its locals are customers (internally share at or above 80%) and it is not boycotting you. The win screen is a ribbon-cutting across the whole world. Confetti falls in your colour, the moon wears your logo if you bought it, and a headline reads 'The Watchdog orders a flat white.' LOSS: the Watchdog's lane reaches the gavel and the case is closed. A rubber stamp thumps 'CASE CLOSED', the map cracks along every border, and each region flips to its own little local shop in its own colour, a patchwork of 48 tiny awnings. Headline: 'Broken into 48 little shops. Locals: Lovely.' That is the only way to lose. There is no bankruptcy, no losing a region for good and no fail state hidden in a sub-system. Both screens show the final map, the time taken ('3 years, 4 months'), a two-line timeline of your lane against the dog's lane, and Try Again (same world and start) or New Game.

### The world

The world is invented: an Earth-like planet with new coastlines and no real geography. It is drawn in a tall 2:3 portrait frame so that on a phone held upright it fills the screen between the top and bottom bars. That frame also gives Apple three portrait screenshots in search instead of one landscape one (research: store page conversion). Climate runs in bands from top to bottom, so it reads at a glance: frozen north, mild rich middle, hot desert and jungle across the centre, farmland below, and a cold southern tip, with island chains scattered along the edges. There are 6 continents and 48 regions, an average of about 69×69 px per region on a 390-wide phone, which is tappable. 1) Frostmark: snowy, rich, few people, 6 regions; ports freeze in winter. 2) Old Ledger: mild, very rich, crowded, 10 regions. It is home to the Watchdog's office (a grey building with columns in its capital), so being loud here gets noticed fastest. 3) Great Sandy: hot desert, poor, huge regions, few roads, 7 regions. 4) Greenreach: tropical coast, crowded, poor, many ports, 9 regions. 5) Breadbasket: farmland, middle wealth, lots of land borders, cold southern tip, 9 regions. 6) Saltspray Isles: 7 remote islands reached only by sea or air, which deliberately tests the 'Greenland problem'. Each region has three plain attributes, shown as icons and words, never numbers: climate (cold / mild / hot / tropical), wealth (rich / middle / poor) and links (land borders, port, airport, remote). Hidden tuning values include population and local loyalty. Regions are market areas, not nations: no governments, flags or armies. The only authority in the world is the fictional Watchdog. Example names, all to be checked against the gazetteer: Frostbuckle, Ledgerton, Pepperdune, Parrotfall, Lower Snorrow, Toffee Shoals, Far Pebble. Why fictional: the owner's rule against real places; Apple 1.1.2 (game 'enemies' may not target a real government); Plague Inc.'s removal from the China App Store; Ndemic's real-Earth trade dress (all three of its games sit in the UK top-20 paid chart); and a portrait world is impossible with real geography. Plague kept the realism players reason with intuitively (cold north, remote islands, rich and poor places), and so does this map. Plague also merged borders for readability (Vaughan, Shacknews); we design for readability from the start.

### What spreads or changes

1) YOUR STRIPES. Each region starts as painted terrain. Where you trade, your brand colour grows outward from its towns in organic blotches. The fill is a candy-stripe shop-awning pattern (colour plus white diagonal stripes), so colour-blind players can read it by pattern. Pale, thin stripes mean you are open there; solid, bold stripes mean the region is Yours. 2) SHOP PINS. Tiny enamel-badge shopfronts pop onto town dots with a ribbon-snip, and they grow as you buy formats: kiosk, café, drive-thru, megastore, flagship tower with a rooftop sign. A region that is Yours gets a giant mascot sign on its capital, and its name label gains your logo badge. 3) ROUTES. Buying a route type draws dashed brand-colour lines across the map in about a second (roads, sea lanes, air arcs, blimp paths), and toy vans, ferries, planes and blimps travel along them. 4) MARKETING. Posters, then billboards, appear in regions you trade in. A jingle sends music-note rings drifting out of your cities. 'Logo on the Moon' puts your logo on the moon in the sky corner. 5) THE WATCHDOG'S MARK. While he is awake, grey inspectors with clipboards walk out of his office along your routes. A region with an inspector in it is wrapped in red tape (a crimson ribbon with a wax seal) and its stripes stop growing. 6) BOYCOTTS. A region that is fed up with you hangs 'Buy Local' bunting across itself, loses colour, and switches off your shop lights. 7) TIME. The sky tint shifts gently with the time of day (about 2 real minutes per cycle at 1x). At dusk the land dims by at most 25% and your shops glow, like a city-lights map in your colour; there is an always-day toggle. Seasons sweep the map as a soft frost line moving down and back up, which matters for products like ice cream.

### Counter force

THE WATCHDOG: one character, one lane, one bar. He starts asleep. He wakes when your noise crosses a threshold, or on a fixed late day, whichever comes first, so doing nothing always loses. Noise is never shown as a number; you read it from his pose. Every loud thing you buy adds noise (Marketing, big shop formats, loud event choices) and quiet things lower it. On screen: in the race strip, the bulldog's sprite has four states: curled asleep with 'zzz', one eye open, hat on with briefcase, sprinting. His lane fills with slate crosshatch, and three wax-seal stamps on the track (Notice, Inquiry, Hearing) thump red as he passes them, each with a bark and a ticker line, before the gavel at the finish. That is our version of Plague's warnings at 25/50/75% (Plague wiki: Cure), in our own words. On the map: once awake, he sends grey inspectors from his office in Old Ledger along your routes into your noisiest regions, and they wrap those regions in red tape, which halves growth there. You can tap an inspector to serve him a coffee: he sits down at your café terrace and does nothing for a while. He is a customer, not a bribe. Scandal events ('Hair found in muffin. Watchdog takes notes.') jump his lane forward visibly. Boycotts are the world's other reaction: regions you have saturated loudly, without Friends upgrades, hang Buy Local bunting. They are never permanent, and a region you have not reached yet can never shut you out (see the Greenland fix under how_a_choice_shows). Rivals appear only in authored scenarios, so the main game keeps exactly one counter-force bar. Rebel Inc.'s many meters were called 'obtuse' and 'opaque' (Pocket Tactics).

### Core loop 10 seconds

Watch your stripes creep and the vans roll. Every 3 to 6 seconds a queue of 3 to 6 tiny people in your colour forms outside one of your shops, bobbing. Tap it: they hop inside and a coin arcs up to your coin pill with a ka-ching. Untapped queues serve themselves after about 6 seconds at 70% value, so tapping is a tip, not a chore. At 2x, at 4x or while the app is closed, queues are collected automatically. About every 30 to 60 seconds a wooden 'For Rent' sign wiggles in a region you have not reached; tap it to open a pop-up shop there for free. Once the Watchdog is awake, tap a walking inspector to sit him down with a coffee. Glance at the dog's pose. At most 3 queues, 1 sign and 4 inspectors are on screen at once, to avoid Rebel Inc.'s 'whack-a-mole' complaint.

### Core loop minute

About once a minute, open HQ and spend coins on one or two upgrades. The real decisions: (1) Reach or deepen: buy Ferries to cross to the next continent, or a menu that wins the climate band you already trade in? (2) Loud or quiet: a Billboard flips regions fast but makes the dog stir; Friendly Staff is slow but keeps him asleep and prevents boycotts. Each node shows 0 to 2 megaphone icons for loudness, or a 'shh' icon for quiet. (3) Defend or push: Very Long Small Print slows the case, but coins spent there do not grow you. (4) Where to send effort: tap a boycotting region and pay for an Apology Tour, or leave it and press on elsewhere. Every purchase raises the price of all later ones a little (Plague's commitment rule, kept), so you cannot buy everything and a build must have a plan. A rare event pops up no more than once every 3 minutes: one sentence and two buttons, each marked loud or quiet. Example: 'A famous chef wants to cook at your Ledgerton café.' Yes (loud) / No thanks (quiet).

### Core loop whole game

One game takes about 20 to 30 minutes at 1x on the default difficulty (target band 15–40 minutes, about half that at 2x). It autosaves every 10 seconds and when you leave, and resumes mid-game at any time. Four acts that the map makes obvious: ACT 1, Grand Opening (0–3 min): one region, first queues, first vans, neighbours light up. ACT 2, Quiet Growth (3–10 min): pick route types and menus to reach every continent while the dog sleeps; pale stripes spread everywhere. This mirrors Plague's 'spread silently first' (Plague wiki), in our own words. ACT 3, The Race (around minute 6–10 onwards): the dog wakes in a short animated beat (stretches, puts on his hat, picks up his briefcase), his lane starts moving, and inspectors walk out. Now you must turn pale regions solid, which means going loud, and loud makes him faster and causes boycotts. ACT 4, Final Push (last 5 min): once 90% of regions are Yours, a 'Final Push' button offers a one-time Launch Party: every region surges for 30 seconds while the dog sprints. Then come the last remote islands, reached by Blimps. It ends in an unmistakable win or loss screen with the map as it finished, which doubles as the share card. 4x speed and Final Push remove Plague's late-game waiting.

### Upgrades

HQ is a five-department tower, not a set of hex tabs. Each department is a column of round 'windows' going up floor by floor, with lines showing prerequisites. There are 40 nodes at launch (8 per department) plus 2 signature nodes per product. Every node has a name, a blurb of at most 50 characters, a loudness icon and a price; no stat tables. Selecting a node previews it on the map before you buy, and buying plays the effect on the map. 1) MENU (who likes you): Iced Drinks (hot bands shimmer and grow faster), Hot Soup (cold bands), Value Menu (poor regions), Posh Cakes (rich regions), Local Recipes (lower loyalty everywhere, quiet), Free Biscuit With Everything, Secret Menu (Yours regions turn faster). On the map, the affected climate or wealth band glows and the shop signs in it change. 2) SHOPS (how big; loud): Kiosk, Café, Drive-Thru, Megastore, Flagship Tower, Self-Service Tills (queues auto-serve at full value). Pins visibly grow and city skylines change; bigger means faster turning and more noise. 3) DELIVERIES (where you can reach): Vans (land borders), Ferries (sea lanes), Cargo Planes (airports), Blimps (any region, including remote islands, ignoring frozen ports), Tunnel Under the Sea (joins two continents; a joke with a real effect). Route lines draw and vehicles set off. 4) MARKETING (fast and loud): Posters, Billboards, Catchy Jingle, TV Advert, Mascot in a Big Costume, Logo on the Moon, and the Launch Party capstone. Posters and billboards sprout, note-rings pulse, the moon gets your logo, and the dog's ears twitch. 5) FRIENDS & PAPERWORK (quiet, and slows the dog): Friendly Staff, Loyalty Stamp Card (prevents boycotts), Sponsor the Village Fete (bunting in your colours; lowers noise), Very Long Small Print (slows the case), Lawyers in Nice Suits (inspectors walk slower), Charity Fun Run (one-time noise drop), Rebrand (big noise reset, once per game), Apology Tour (unlocks the tap action that reopens a boycotting region). Effects resolve through the reused modifier resolver (additions before multiplications), so the order of purchase never matters.

### How a choice shows

The rule: a purchase changes the map within 1 second and changes the spread within about 10 seconds at 1x. No score number is ever shown. Examples: (1) The player taps Ferries, then Buy. The HQ sheet drops away at once. Dashed sea lanes draw from every port you trade from (0.8 s each) and tiny ferries set out. Within about 8 seconds at 1x the first island across the sea gets a ribbon-snip and a shop pin, and pale stripes start round its town. The receipt prints 'Ferry passengers report: free biscuits.' (2) The player taps Billboards (two megaphones). Billboards pop up in every region you trade in within 1 second, and over the next 5 seconds the stripes there visibly thicken. ### Tone and humour

The voice is a cheerful company brochure that is a little too pleased with itself, set against a world that answers with dry, understated jokes. The Watchdog is a lovable stickler, never a villain; locals are sensible, never stupid; nobody real and no real brand is mocked. Jokes live in short headlines (at most 60 characters) and upgrade names, never in paragraphs. As the dog's lane fills, the jokey headlines thin out and tense ones take over, which reuses Plague's 'flavour headlines fade' rhythm (Plague wiki: News). Examples: 'Local gran rates your scone: acceptable.' / 'Frostbuckle man queues 3 hours, forgets why.' / 'Experts: it is just a muffin. Queues lengthen.' / 'Watchdog buys a second filing cabinet.' / 'Watchdog: we are close.' / 'Mayor renames high street after your logo.' Win: 'Everyone is now a customer. The Watchdog orders a flat white.' Loss: 'Broken into 48 little shops. Locals: Lovely.' There is no gore and no violence, and nobody loses their job on screen. The losing ending is a happy one for the locals.

### Art direction

The look is an illustrated toy map in daylight, a painted tourist map crossed with a toy-shop window. This answers TheSixthAxis's 'isn't much of a looker' verdict on Plague and the owner judging with his eyes. Land is gouache-painted terrain with soft paper grain and thin ink coastlines (#2A2F38). Towns are tiny isometric clusters. The sea is calm and deep with drifting wave lines. Your brand is always a candy-stripe awning pattern that grows as organic blotches: a fixed noise texture thresholded against each region's share in the shader. Shop pins look like chunky enamel badges; vehicles are toy vans, ferries, planes and blimps. The Watchdog is a round bulldog in a bowler hat and tweed waistcoat, drawn flat-shaded in the same style; inspectors are small grey figures with clipboards. The UI is cream paper, ink and gold coins, with rounded chunky buttons and a slight bevel. Use one bundled, subset OFL display font (about 25 KB woff2, self-hosted, a deliberate exception to the no-webfont stance) for the title, region names and buttons, and system fonts for everything else. The restraint reads as premium, following Vaughan's 'clean, dispassionate' lesson: no dark red, no black, no hex grids, no real-Earth outline. PALETTE: sea #2C5D7C (day) to #1B3550 (dusk); land paper #F1E6CC, meadow #B9C99A, forest #7E9C74, desert #E3BF86, snow #F4F6F7, rock #A89F91; UI paper #FBF5E9, ink #22303C, coin gold #E9B949; Watchdog slate #5D6B7A, red tape #B8323A (ribbon only, never a fill). Brand choices, always drawn with white stripes: Tomato #E5533D, Sunflower #F2B632, Mint #23B58A, Sky #2F86DE, Grape #7B57D1, Bubblegum #EC6FA6. References in words: Mini Metro's clarity, Townscaper's toy charm, 1950s airline route maps, Richard Scarry's busy towns, a café chalkboard for HQ labels. A small team can build this in WebGL2: one full-screen shader (terrain texture, a region-ID lookup, the stripe threshold, border glow from the distance field, dusk tint), instanced sprites for pins and vehicles, and DOM elements for the things you tap. Signature animations: ribbon-snip, awning unrolling, the dog waking, the stamp thump, the map shattering, confetti, and the moon logo.

### Replay and content

PRODUCTS (our version of Plague's 'types'): winning with one product unlocks the next, and each bends the rules and the map. 1 Café (free starter, balanced). 2 Ice Cream (booms in the hot band; seasons sweep the map and it stalls in winter). 3 Fizzy Pop (very fast and very loud; the dog wakes early). 4 Street Food (land borders only until late; islands become the puzzle). 5 Gadget Shop (starts only in rich regions and jumps by air; poor regions need the Budget Model). 6 Gym (locals drift away unless you keep them; a New Year surge). 7 Delivery App (no shops; a region turns when your routes surround it, a network puzzle). 8 Theme Park (one giant park per continent; tourists fly your brand home; slow start, explosive middle). DIFFICULTIES, named for the dog: Puppy, Terrier, Bulldog, Bloodhound (never fully asleep). 8 products × 4 difficulties = 32 wins to collect. SCENARIOS: 12 authored rule twists at launch, then 4 per update. ### Fixes couldnt tell what to do

1) The goal is one sentence printed at the top of the screen all game, and progress is a literal two-lane race: your van against the bulldog, first to the finish wins. Anyone who has seen a race understands it. 2) The screen is capped at six things (map, race strip, ticker, coins, HQ, speed). Apart from prices, the only numbers anywhere are your coins and '19/48'. There are no meters, multipliers or score readouts. Noise shows as the dog's pose and a region's mood as words. 3) Every rule is shown on the map rather than explained. Cold regions shimmer when soup helps them, red tape shows where inspectors slow you, and bunting shows a boycott. 4) The first minute is five taps, with coach lines of at most 6 words and no rules screen. 5) Loudness is a single icon on every upgrade (megaphones or 'shh'), which teaches the game's one trade-off. 6) Enforced in code: the validator caps headlines at 60 characters, blurbs at 50 and event text at 120, rejects digits and percentage signs in blurbs (words, not numbers), and fails the build if a HUD element is added beyond the cap list.

### Fixes choices felt pointless

1) No choice ever produces a score. Every purchase has a visible map effect within 1 second and a spread effect within about 10 seconds at 1x: routes draw, bands glow, pins grow, billboards sprout, the dog opens an eye. A Playwright test hook measures the time from purchase to the first new tinted region, and CI fails if it exceeds 15 s at 1x. 2) Previews: selecting a node shows on the map where it will act before you spend anything. 3) The choices are real forks with costs: reach versus deepen, loud versus quiet, push versus defend. Each purchase raises later prices, so builds commit. 4) Outcomes differ visibly: a quiet build has a sleeping dog and pale stripes everywhere; a loud build has solid stripes, red tape and bunting. 5) The balance simulator enforces that choices matter. Quiet-first and loud-rush bot policies must both win within 10 points of the balanced bot on Terrier; no single upgrade above tier 1 may appear in more than 35% of winning builds; the idle bot must lose 100% of games; fewer than 15% of games may be decided by minute 10.

### Fixes looked unfinished

1) Art comes first. Before any code, commission a main-screen mockup and a 20-second animatic and show them to the owner. The commissioned assets are one hand-painted world (3–4 weeks), a character and prop sheet (dog, inspectors, queues, pins, vehicles; about 2 weeks) and a UI kit (1 week). No channel ships, and no Steam page goes up, with placeholder art. 2) A build gate: the validator fails production builds on missing asset ids, 'TODO', 'lorem', placeholder textures, untranslated keys or over-length text. 3) A list of signature animations and sounds (ribbon-snip, awning unroll, dog wake, stamp thump, map shatter, confetti, ka-ching) plus commissioned or generative café music for 30-minute sessions. 4) There are no walls of text anywhere: the longest text in the game is a 120-character event line. 5) Polish is tested: 60 fps on a mid-range Android phone from week 2, a quality tier for low-end devices, Playwright screenshot baselines of the map at fixed ticks, and recovery from WebGL context loss so the map never comes back black. 6) The store's first screenshot and the first 3 seconds of the preview video are the main screen mid-game at dusk with the lights glowing, and the icon is the globe wearing an awning.

### Fixes wrong for market

1) The format is the one the owner chose and the one with proven reach: a map, colour spreading, one counter-force and a race. Plague Inc. reached 170M+ players and stayed in the paid charts for years. 2) The theme is universal and instantly understood, 'a café on every corner', a pattern every adult has seen on their own high street. That gives a one-sentence pitch as clear as 'Can you infect the world?' while flipping the usual small-business-hero story, as Plague flipped 'save the world'. 3) It is the widest possible audience: no violence, disease or war, rated about 4+ on Apple, PEGI 3 and ESRB E. That passes Apple 1.1.2 and 1.1.7 and Google's sensitive-events policy, and it is advertisable on TikTok and Meta, where war imagery is restricted. 4) It is phone-first, because phones are the wide base (comparable map games have about 60x more Google Play reviews than Steam reviews). The portrait world gives three screenshots in App Store search, one-handed play, and resume at any time. 5) Prices sit inside each channel's proven band: a free, finishable first game; a £3.99 one-time unlock; $9.99 on Steam; a web demo as the funnel. No ads and no grind keeps it 'premium strategy', Ndemic's positioning. 6) The share card shows the finished world in your colours and gives nothing away. It is the word-of-mouth engine Plague relied on.

### Distance from plague inc

What players see is different everywhere: an invented, painted, portrait-shaped world in daylight, not a dark real-Earth map with red and black dots. Spread is a striped awning pattern, not dots. The counter-force is a character with agents walking the map, not a research percentage. The layout is flipped: a two-lane race strip at the top, a till-receipt ticker at the bottom, HQ as a centre button and a five-department tower, not three hex tabs. Pickups are queues of tiny people, For Rent signs and inspectors, never bubbles. Copy rules enforced by the validator ban 'Inc', 'DNA', 'evolve', 'cure', 'infect', 'plague', 'symptom', 'transmission', 'abilities' and 'Mega-Brutal'. Difficulties are dog names, the win is everyone happily buying, and the loss is being broken into local shops. Honest premise risk: press and players will still call it 'Plague Inc, but a coffee chain'. That is a usable hook, but it caps critical reception if execution is merely derivative (Bio Inc. Redemption scores 57 on OpenCritic). Apple guideline 4.1 copycat review is low risk given the different trade dress, but not zero. A separate neighbour: Coffee Inc 2 ('Build Your Global Coffee Empire', App Store and Apple Arcade) is a menu-driven management tycoon, not a map race. It shows why the title and store keywords must avoid 'coffee', 'empire' and 'Inc'; the starter product can stay a café.

### Sensitivity risks

1) Corporate satire: some players may read it as celebrating monopoly, others as anti-business. Handled by the gentle tone, a lovable Watchdog, and a losing ending that is happy for locals. No real companies, regulators or people, and no jokes about workers or layoffs. 2) Bribery and corruption: none. Inspectors are served coffee as paying customers; 'Lawyers' and 'Small Print' joke about bureaucracy. The validator forbids 'bribe', 'kickback' and 'lobby'. 3) Food and children: products are fictional and mixed (there is a Gym), with no health claims, no 'addictive' or 'hooked' copy, and no real food brands. Caffeine is mentioned only as 'café'. 4) Brand lookalikes: logos come from a library of abstract shapes, avoiding round green emblems, arches and similar marks. Extend FORBIDDEN_WORDS with major brand names and run a gazetteer check on all region names, including the GeoNames list. 5) Trademark proximity: 'Watchdog' is a generic word used only inside the game, but keep it out of the title and store name because of Ubisoft's Watch Dogs; clear 'Every Corner' before buying the icon art. 6) No gambling-like mechanics: no loot boxes, no random paid items, one honest unlock. 7) Ratings: aim for Apple 4+ under the new questionnaire, PEGI 3, ESRB E (IARC on Google). 8) Localisation: puns need care per market. Mainland China is not targeted (licensing).

### Biggest risks

- Owner taste, fourth attempt. The owner's original idea was a nuclear crisis, and a cheerful café takeover may again feel 'not what's in my head'. Mitigation: show the main-screen mockup and a 20-second animatic before any code. The simulation is theme-agnostic (regions, spread, one counter-force), so a reskin costs art and copy, not engine work.
- Lower stakes than Plague's dark flip. Turning the world your colour may not deliver Plague's catharsis ('heartbreaking and cathartic'), and there will be no news-driven spikes like Ebola or COVID. Comedy has to carry word of mouth, and comedy is subjective and hard to localise.
- Clone perception: 'Plague Inc, but coffee' will be the press shorthand. It is a hook, but critics mark down derivative work (Bio Inc. 57 on OpenCritic) and the bar for depth and polish is high.
- Crowded keyword space: idle and tycoon 'empire' games and Coffee Inc 2 own the business searches. Discovery depends on the screenshot, the icon and share cards, and premium-style mobile is only about 4% of downloads.
- Free-to-paid conversion is unknown (the old benchmark is 7–10%). With no ads, revenue rests entirely on one unlock and later world packs.
- Continuous-simulation balance: the middle game can sag or be decided too early, and small parameter changes swing results. Bots cannot judge feel, so human playtests are needed every sprint.
- Three tappable types (queues, signs, inspectors) could become Rebel Inc.'s 'whack-a-mole'. The caps and auto-collect must be tuned with real players.
- Art dependency: the whole pitch rests on one excellent hand-painted world plus character art. Mediocre commissioned art repeats complaint (c), and each World pack repeats the cost.
- Performance and battery: 30-minute WebGL sessions on mid-range phones; WebKitGTK on Steam Deck under Tauri; WebGL context loss when the app is backgrounded.
- Cross-engine determinism for the Daily Continent: the simulation must use only fixed-point maths, with state hashes checked in Chromium, WebKit and Firefox.
- A portrait-shaped world leaves sea margins on widescreen PC, and Steam players may want a bigger map. Wider worlds can come as World packs.
- Satire mis-read, or an accidental resemblance between a logo or name and a real brand or place.

## NEVERSPRING (working title; one constant, BRAND.name in src/config.ts). In October 2026 a web search found no commercial game with this name. The only similar results were two unfinished game-jam games, "War For Winter" and "Ro'y: Heart of Winter". A trademark check (UK IPO, EUIPO, USPTO, store search) is still needed before committing.

### One line

You are a winter that will not end: cover the whole world in snow before its people light the great Furnace that would bring back spring.

### Player role

You are the winter itself, the first real cold to return after a thousand warm years. You have no face, no character and no speeches; you are the white on the map. The people of the world have no faces either. You only see them as warm city lights, ships and trains carrying fuel, and the giant Furnace they are building to end you. When the player starts their second game they pick a name for their winter from a generated list ("The Long Hush", "Old White", "The Quiet Year"). There is no free-text box, so nothing user-written ever reaches a share card.

### Goal

Freeze the world before the Furnace is lit. This line is always printed on the race strip above the map, over two bars: Frozen (white) and Furnace (amber).

### Win and loss

WIN: every land region is frozen before the Furnace bar reaches 100%. That means snow over every region, every Hearth out and the last city light dark. When the last light goes out, aurora ribbons circle the world and a title card reads "Neverspring. Year 6." LOSS: the Furnace bar reaches 100%. The Furnace lights, a gold ring sweeps inward from it across the whole map, and the snow melts to green in 3 seconds. The card reads "Spring came." with one line, "You froze 81% of the world." EDGE LOSS: all of your snow melts in a summer. This can only happen if you start somewhere hot and then do nothing. The card reads "Your winter melted." There is no score. Both end screens show the final map, a small two-line timeline (white line against amber line, by year) and a Share button.

### The world

The world is fictional, seen from straight above its north pole and painted as one ROUND map, a disc. The cold is at the centre and the warmth at the rim, so the whole game reads as one picture: white growing outward from the middle to the edge. It has 48 land regions on 5 landmasses, plus 9 sea zones that can freeze.

Landmasses (plain-word names):
(1) THE NORTHLANDS, 12 regions around the polar sea: tundra and pine, few people, easy to freeze, the usual start.
(2) THE HEARTLAND, 11 regions in the middle ring: farms, rivers and rich cities. Most of the Furnace's supply lines start here.
(3) THE HIGH COUNTRY, 7 regions: a spine of mountains running from near the pole out to the rim. It is the "snow road" into the hot lands.
(4) THE SUNLANDS, 12 regions along the outer rim: desert, jungle and warm coasts. They are the hardest to freeze and usually where the Furnace is built.
(5) THE SCATTERED ISLES, 6 islands in a warm western ocean, reached by drift ice, icebergs or wind.

Sea zones include the Polar Sea (centre), the Narrows (a strait between the Northlands and the Heartland), the Inner Sea, the Western Ocean, the Sun Sea, and the Warm Current, a warm tongue of sea that reaches inward and resists freezing. Regions get short invented names (for example Hask, Ostrel, Toradel, Sel Harbour, Velloran, Mirrin). Every name passes the validator's gazetteer and trademark check.

Each region has six plain attributes: climate (polar, cold, mild, dry, hot, wet), height (low, hills, high), people (few, some, many), wealth (poor, middling, rich), coasts, and neighbours (computed from the art).

WHY FICTIONAL AND ROUND:
- It meets the owner's no-real-states rule and Apple 1.1.2.
- It brings no border disputes.
- It is not Plague's flat real-Earth trade dress.
- A polar view makes the theme self-explaining: cold middle, warm edge.
- A circle fits a portrait phone, a landscape PC and a Steam Deck equally well, with no re-layout of the map.

It keeps the intuitive realism Plague relies on: cold north, hot rim, mountains hold snow, deserts resist, islands are remote, rich lands build faster.

### What spreads or changes

YOURS (white and ice blue):
- Snow creeps over land as soft, organic blotches with blue shadows, not flat fills.
- Seas crack over into pale pack ice, which makes ice bridges.
- Wind ribbons you buy appear as flowing white streamlines.
- Icebergs drift visibly to far coasts.
- Blizzards you drop swirl on the spot.
- Ice crystals glint on newly frozen ground.
- The seasons breathe: each winter the snow line pushes outward and each summer thin snow retreats toward the centre, so the whole disc pulses once a year.

THEIRS (amber and gold):
- City lights glow warmer and brighter as the world grows alarmed, then go dark one by one as regions freeze.
- Hearths are amber glass domes that snap on over cities that dig in.
- Supply lines are amber dotted arcs with moving glints (ships and trains) running toward the Furnace. They reroute around new ice, turn grey when cut, and vanish when their source region freezes.
- The Furnace is a gold star on the rim with a column of light that brightens and pulses faster as it nears 100%.

LATE GAME: at 80% Frozen, the Long Night. The sky darkens at the rim and the remaining lights cluster and flicker.

### Counter force

THE FURNACE. There is one bar, and it is the only counter-force.

1. Before it starts: the world has not noticed. The Furnace slot shows an eye icon instead of a bar, and the eye opens in three steps (closed, half, open). Snow in mild or rich regions, storms, and time all open it. Upgrades in the Quiet arm slow it.

2. When the eye opens, "the world wakes". The camera nudges to the Furnace site for 3 seconds, a beam of gold light rises, and the radio reads: "Nations agree: build the Great Furnace in Toradel." The site is seeded per game, usually the richest warm region on the rim.

3. Furnace speed comes from what you can see. It burns slowly on its own, and every amber supply line feeding it makes it faster. Lines come from unfrozen, rich or populous regions. Each line takes the shortest path through unfrozen land and open sea, so freezing a strait, river or region on its path cuts or reroutes it in front of you. The eye icon is replaced by a flame whose size (small, medium, roaring) shows the speed. Cutting a line visibly shrinks the flame.

4. HEARTHS are local resistance. In alarmed regions, cities light amber domes that hold back snow and keep the region feeding the Furnace. A Hearth cut off from every supply path flickers and goes out after about a season. Isolation helps YOU, which is the opposite of Plague's closed borders. Storms and the "Chill Through Walls" crystal also crack Hearths.

5. SUMMER is the world's ally: thin snow melts back unless you have locked it with Ice upgrades.

6. Cut every line and the Furnace slows to a crawl, and its own city can then freeze. That puts it out and almost always wins the game.

### Core loop 10 seconds

Watch the snow creep. Tap the ice crystals that grow on newly frozen land, and on spots where you cut a supply line or put out a Hearth. The crystal shatters and its sparkles fly into the Grow button as Frost, the one currency.

An untapped crystal is never wasted. After about 8 seconds it melts into the ground as a little extra local snow, so tapping is a bonus, not a chore. At 4x speed crystals collect themselves.

When the Storm button has a charge (it holds 2, then 3 with an upgrade, and recharges one per in-game month, which is 15 seconds at 1x), drag the cloud onto the map and let go. A blizzard swirls there and freezes that land or sea within 3 to 4 seconds. Storms are loud: nearby city lights flare and the eye opens a notch.

Glance at the radio line at the bottom. Tapping a headline flies the camera to the place it names.

### Core loop minute

Five recurring decisions, all made on the map:
(1) GROW: which arm of the Snowflake to grow next. Reach (Sky, Sea), strength (Snow, Ice) or stealth and stalling (Quiet, Dark). Each crystal makes every later one cost a little more, so you commit to a shape.
(2) AIM: where to throw a storm. Grow coverage, freeze a strait to cut the fattest supply line, or hit a Hearth before it settles.
(3) QUIET OR LOUD: storms and fast freezing win ground now but wake the world sooner and make the Furnace start earlier.
(4) SEASON: push hard in winter. Before summer, lock key regions with Ice upgrades so the melt only takes thin edges.
(5) WHICH LINE: tap the Furnace flame and every line feeding it lights up, thickest first. Decide which one to cut and how (sea ice, frozen river, freeze the source region).

### Core loop whole game

YEAR 1, QUIET: pick where winter begins (the default start is late autumn, so the first minutes feel like growth). Snow creeps, nobody notices, and the radio jokes ("Ski shops in Ostrel sell out by breakfast").

YEARS 2 TO 3, THE WORLD WAKES: the eye opens, the Furnace is founded with a beam of light, supply lines spring out across the map, and the first Hearths snap on.

YEARS 3 TO 6, THE RACE: cut lines, crack Hearths, survive summers, push the white out to the rim. The two bars race each other.

FROZEN 80%, THE LONG NIGHT: the rim darkens, unfrozen land cools steadily, Hearths gutter faster, and the Furnace makes its final push. The tension drone rises and a heartbeat pulse starts once the Furnace passes 90%. A "Skip to the end" 8x button appears.

END: the last light goes out (win) or the Furnace lights (loss).

LENGTH: one in-game month is 15 seconds at 1x and a year is 3 minutes. A typical game runs 6 to 9 years: 18 to 27 minutes at 1x, or 10 to 15 minutes played mostly at 2x and 4x. The game autosaves every 10 seconds and on pagehide, and resumes mid-month on any device. A first game always ends well inside Steam's 2-hour refund window.

### Upgrades

THE SNOWFLAKE (button label "Grow").

The upgrade tree is one six-armed snowflake. The centre, "First Frost", is owned at the start. There are three branches of two arms each, and five crystals per arm: 30 crystals in all. A typical game grows 15 to 20 of them, so no two winters have the same shape. The finished snowflake is a picture of your build and is printed on the share card.

COSTS AND RULES: first-tier crystals cost about 4 Frost and fifth-tier about 20 to 28. Every crystal you own adds 2 to the cost of all the others. You can melt a crystal back for half its cost. Every blurb is one line of 60 characters or fewer with no digits; the validator enforces this. Selecting a crystal previews its effect on the map behind the sheet (a ghost wind line, tinted regions, glowing seas) before you buy.

WIND (pale cyan): reach
- SKY: North Wind (snow drifts into neighbouring lands), Storm Front (one more storm cloud), Jet Stream (aim a river of wind across an ocean; you drag its end once), Snow Clouds (snow can settle on warm land), Endless Gale (winds keep blowing through summer).
- SEA: Cold Current (frozen coasts chill the coasts across the water), Drift Ice (floes carry snow to islands), Pack Ice (seas beside frozen land freeze; ships turn back), Icebergs (bergs drift out and freeze far coasts), Frozen Ocean (even open ocean turns to ice).

COLD (white): strength
- SNOW: Heavy Snow (snow piles up faster), Blizzard (storms grow bigger), Mountain Snow (snow races along high ground), Snow in the Sand (snow settles in dry lands), White Sky (a little snow falls everywhere).
- ICE: Hard Frost (frozen land melts less in summer), Frozen Rivers (rivers freeze and cut inland lines), Black Ice (roads and rails slow; every line weakens), Chill Through Walls (Hearths burn out faster), Deep Freeze (frozen land never thaws again).

HUSH (lavender): stealth and stalling
- QUIET: Mild Days (the world is slower to notice), Just a Cold Snap (experts dismiss your first year), Soft Snow (storms make less noise), Lost Forecasts (the world forgets you a little each summer), Silent Winter (Hearths are slower to light).
- DARK: Short Days (winters start early and end late), Grey Skies (summer sun barely melts your snow), Cold Summer (summers grow short and cool), Year Without Summer (the next summer never comes), Dim Sun (the sun fades; the Furnace burns slower).

HOW THE MAP CHANGES: wind arms draw new routes, Sea makes seas turn white and lets bergs drift, Snow makes white spread faster and shows on the mountain spines, Ice gives frozen regions a crisp blue rim that does not retreat in summer, Quiet keeps city lights dim and the eye closed, and Dark tints the rim toward dusk while the flame shrinks.

### Art direction

"A painted dusk atlas of a round world, coming to life." It is a single painted illustration that a small team can make beautiful in WebGL2.

BASE ART: one commissioned round painting, 4096px for desktop and 2048px for phones, with a gouache or watercolour feel:
- ochre plains, deep-green pine, tan deserts, slate mountains with soft hill shading;
- ink-teal ocean with gentle depth bands and rivers in pale lines;
- a permanent blue-hour dusk, so warm city lights read clearly.

SHADER LAYERS:
- Snow: a noise texture thresholded against each region's frost value. It gives organic edges, blue shadows #9CC9E8 under white #F2F7FB, and sparse sparkle specks.
- Frozen seas: a pale cracked-ice texture.
- Wind: thin white streamlines at about 30% opacity.
- City lights: amber points #F2A23A with soft bloom.
- Hearths: amber glass rings with bloom.
- Supply lines: dotted amber arcs with travelling glints.
- Furnace: gold star #FFD27A with a light column whose pulse quickens toward 100%.
- Aurora on a win (green-teal ribbons) and a gold thaw wave on a loss.
- Crimson #E4573D appears only on the Furnace bar above 90%.

UI: ink panels #0F1E2E, 1px frost borders, rounded system sans (no web fonts), simple line icons. Colour has one meaning across the whole game: white and blue are you, amber and gold are them. That pair is safe for red-green colour-blindness, and the two also differ strongly in brightness. Snow carries a texture and Hearths a ring shape, so nothing relies on hue alone.

REFERENCES IN WORDS: an antique polar-projection atlas brought to life (but not the UN emblem: no laurel, no flat blue-on-white); Mini Metro's restraint; city lights seen from orbit, but painted; the calm of a snow globe; the warm-cold light of Alto's Odyssey.

MOTION AND QUALITY: reduced-motion mode swaps the swirls for static arrows. A low-power tier caps the pixel ratio at 1.5, frame rate at 30fps and drops bloom.

  - fewer than 30% of games are effectively decided by minute 10.

### Fixes looked unfinished

- The whole art budget goes on one commissioned painted world that is the product's face, plus a small set of hand-tuned animations: dome snap-on, line reroute, crystal shatter, sea crack, gold thaw wave, aurora win.
- No shipped build may contain placeholder art. A CI check fails the build if any asset id in the manifest is marked placeholder.
- Text is minimal and lives in one radio line.
- There is real sound: wind bed, crystal chimes, Hearth thump, a drone tied to the Furnace, and commissioned ambient music.
- Step zero, before any engine code: a final-quality static mockup of the portrait main screen plus a 20-second animated clip, for the owner to judge by eye. That same image becomes store screenshot #1 and the first 3 seconds of the preview video.
- Quality tiers keep 60fps on mid-range phones. A WebGL context loss restores cleanly instead of leaving a black map.

### Fixes wrong for market

- WINTER NEEDS NO EXPLAINING: everyone knows winter from fairy tales and films, and "be the winter that never ends" is understood in one second, the way Plague borrowed from Contagion.
- IT IS STORE-SAFE: no disease (Apple 1.1.7, the China removal, the COVID game bans), no war or nuclear theme (TikTok and Meta ad limits), no real governments (Apple 1.1.2). The target ratings are about Apple 9+ and PEGI 7, which widens the base to families and makes paid ads possible.
- THE NICHE IS EMPTY: a web search found only unfinished game-jam games about playing as winter, which matches Vaughan's "fill an empty niche".
- IT USES A PROVEN SHAPE AND PRICES: the Plague formula of map, spread, one counter-bar and an upgrade tree, at Plague, Rebel and Polytopia price points: free plus £3.99 on phone, $9.99 on Steam, and a free web demo as the funnel.
- IT HAS A FREE YEARLY NEWS HOOK: Plague's spikes came from outbreak news. Ours come from every cold snap and first-snow day, which are harmless, seasonal and repeat every year. The Nov to Feb marketing window is predictable.
- IT IS SHAREABLE: the end card is a white world plus your unique snowflake, made for screenshots.

### Distance from plague inc

MECHANICAL INSPIRATION ONLY: spread on a map, a currency spent on upgrades, a race against one counter-force. Everything a player or reviewer sees is different:
- Premise: weather, not disease. There are no hosts, infection, death counts, DNA, cure or 'evolve'.
- Map: an invented ROUND polar world painted at dusk, against Plague's flat real Earth with red dots.
- Player verb: you aim storms directly by dragging, and you aim the Jet Stream. Plague has no direct targeting.
- Counter-force: a physical Furnace on the map fed by visible supply lines you cut, not an abstract research meter.
- Isolation helps you: cut-off Hearths gutter out. This is the opposite of Plague's border closures and avoids its Greenland problem by design.
- Seasons make the snow line breathe yearly.
- Upgrade tree: a six-armed snowflake that grows as you buy, not three hex tabs.
- Pickups are ice crystals that melt into local snow if ignored, not bubbles that vanish.
- Layout: the race strip is at the top, the dock is bottom-centre, the headlines are a bottom radio line, and there are no 'World' or 'Disease' buttons.
- The name has no "Inc."
- Marketing avoids the "Can you X the world?" phrasing. The line is "Be the winter that never ends."

REMAINING RISK: the genre is shared, so some reviews will still say "Plague Inc with snow". Apple 4.1 copycat review risk is low given a different premise, art, layout and wording, but the store page must lead with the round world and the storms.

### Sensitivity risks

1. IMPLIED HARM FROM COLD: the win implies a world lost to winter. This is handled abstractly: no one dies on screen and there are no people counters. People "stay in" or "head for the coast", lights simply go dark, and the win line is "It is quiet everywhere." The lint bans death and injury words.
2. REAL COLD-WEATHER TRAGEDIES AND ENERGY CRISES: no fuel brands, bills or energy politics. A marketing rule pauses paid ads during deadly real cold waves. This is not a "current event" under Apple 1.1.7, but tone matters.
3. CLIMATE READINGS: it is not a climate game and uses no climate vocabulary. The jokes target everyday denial ("Nothing to worry about"), never real science or policy. Some critics may still read it as allegory; the fairy-tale register and fictional world keep that low.
4. DISNEY'S FROZEN: no ice queen or any character. The icon is a snowflake on a disc. The copy contains no "let it go" puns or franchise names, and the lint bans them.
5. CHILDREN: the theme will attract kids. That is acceptable here because the game has no accounts, ads, free text, chat, profiling or cookies, and its analytics are cookie-free, which fits the UK Children's Code. Do not enter Apple's Kids category, which has stricter rules; rate it 9+.
6. GREY SKY WINTER: the volcanic winter uses a fictional mountain only; no real eruptions are referenced.
7. FIRE IMAGERY: the Furnace is warm and hopeful. No cities burn.
8. NAME AND TRADEMARK: Neverspring needs a formal trademark and store search.

### Biggest risks

- The owner's taste: four designs have been rejected. Winter as the premise, or a round map instead of a flat one, may still not be the picture in his head. Mitigation: before any engine code, show him one final-quality painted main screen and a 20-second animated clip. The engine (spread against a Furnace fed by cuttable supply lines) does not depend on the theme, so a different skin would cost art, not code.
- Clone perception: some reviewers will call it 'Plague Inc with snow', and Apple guideline 4.1 allows copycat rejections. Mitigation: lead every store asset with what is ours (the round world, dragging storms, cutting the Furnace's supply lines, the snowflake tree), and keep Plague's vocabulary out of all copy.
- The hook may be softer than Plague's dark thrill. Freezing the world is gentler than wiping out humanity, so some core strategy players may shrug while families lean in. Test the hook with Apple's product page tests, Google Play listing experiments, and the web demo's 60-second retention (target 80% or more).
- The picture is the pitch. The commissioned map painting is estimated at £3k to £8k and music at £2k to £5k (my estimates). The snow shader and bloom must hold 60fps on a mid-range Android phone, and the Tauri build on Steam Deck runs on WebKitGTK, which has the weakest WebGL of the three engines. Test on real devices from week 2.
- Balance in a continuous, chaotic simulation: storms could dominate or be ignored, and cutting supply lines could snowball so that games are decided early. The late game could still drag. Mitigation: bots with hard targets, a parameter sweep tool, the Long Night phase plus a skip button, and human playtests every two weeks.
- Small tap targets on a portrait phone: the average region is about 45px across on a 390px disc, and centre regions are smaller. Mitigation: pinch zoom, region lookup from the ID mask (so any point in a region counts), crystals with 44px hit areas, and storms that do not need precision. Test with real thumbs early.
- Seasonality and discovery: interest may dip in the northern summer. Premium mobile games are about 4% of downloads, and we have no ad budget and no tracking. The plan rests on screenshots, word of mouth, Steam wishlists (5k to 7k before launch), Next Fest, and a November launch.
- Scope: 16 to 22 developer-weeks plus commissioned art and music. The native shells have never been built, and there are three separate purchase paths (Stripe, StoreKit and Play Billing, Steam), each needing its own tests. iOS needs a Mac.
- Determinism across browser engines: the Daily Winter and replays break if the simulation uses Math.exp or Math.pow. The simulation must use integer maths and lookup tables only, with state hashes compared in Chromium, WebKit and Firefox.
- The name may already be taken: a formal trademark search is still needed.

### Build scope

STEP 0 (1 week): a final-quality portrait mockup of the main screen and a 20-second animated clip, for the owner's sign-off.

VERTICAL SLICE, WEB ONLY (6 to 8 weeks): one painted disc, 18 crystals, storms, the Furnace with supply lines, Hearths, seasons, the radio line, a win and a loss.

FULL RELEASE ON PHONE, STEAM AND WEB: about 16 to 22 developer-weeks, plus a commissioned map painting (about 3 weeks of an illustrator's time) and music.

REUSED AS IS:
- the seeded xoshiro RNG, with fork() giving separate streams for spread, alarm, Hearths, events, headlines and bots;
- safe local persistence;
- the web unlock token client and the Stripe Worker (configuration only, and the 'endless' to 'full' rename in three files);
- the Web Audio core (drone tied to Furnace %, heartbeat pulse above 90%);
- content hot-reload;
- the itch.io zip build.

REUSED WITH CHANGES:
- YAML loader and validator: new folders regions/, seas.yaml, upgrades/ (the Snowflake), winters/, scenarios/, headlines/ and rules.yaml (difficulty). It cross-checks map.json and adds a gazetteer, Plague-word and Frozen-word lint, the 60-character limit, and the no-digits rule for blurbs.
- Modifier resolver: becomes the crystal-effect resolver (spread by climate and height, melt, alarm, storm size, line strength, Hearth burn).
- Condition evaluator: now reads Frozen %, Furnace %, season, owned crystals and region state.
- Simulator CLI: about 70% rewritten, with the targets listed under the choices fix.
- Bot policies: idle, random, rush, quiet and line-cutter.
- Also adapted: daily seeding (Daily Winter record), the share card (final disc drawn from the ID mask plus the snowflake), the OG image, the PWA (orientation 'any', larger precache limit), Tauri (1280x720, steamworks crate for achievements and Cloud), Capacitor (both orientations, native IAP, share and preferences), the Playwright test hook with screenshot baselines, theme tokens, and CI with a cross-engine state-hash check.

BUILT NEW:
- tools/map-build.ts: turns the authored round SVG into a crisp-edged region-ID mask, terrain textures at 2048 and 4096, a distance field, adjacency, river and sea paths, and map.json.
- WebGL2 renderer: one full-screen quad and a 64x1 state texture (frost, Hearth, light, highlight per cell). Snow is a noise threshold, lines and glints are instanced quads, labels are DOM, with a Canvas 2D fallback.
- Camera and input: touch, mouse, keys and gamepad.
- Simulation core: pure, fixed-tick and deterministic. One tick is one in-game day; 1x is 2 ticks per second. Integer frost of 0 to 1000 per cell in typed arrays. Integer alarm and Furnace values. Supply lines recomputed by BFS over the 57 cells only when a cell's frozen state changes. Storms, crystals and wind routes are lists. A command log of (tick, action) entries drives replays. Commands: start, collect, grow, melt, storm, aimWind, setSpeed.
- Snowflake UI, radio-line director, HUD, Daily Winter, Snow Globe Shelf, and the three store purchase integrations.
- The card engine, store.ts, policies and card content packs are retired, not adapted.

HARDEST PART: making the painted disc and the snow shader look like the mockup at 60fps on a mid-range phone, and tuning the simulation so that a storm or a cut line feels decisive without deciding the whole game.
